Our Mission

Liminal Lit is an advocacy focused space for writers navigating the complexities of multiple identities. We exist because the most powerful stories often live in the "in-between" — the friction of existing at the intersection of different cultures, religions, languages, or the experience of being a minority in your own community.

Whether you are first generation, third generation, or simply navigating a lack of belonging, we want your raw perspectives. We do this to dismantle the barriers in traditional publishing and provide a dedicated platform for diaspora and marginalized voices to be heard, celebrated, and compensated.

Liminality is not just about geography. It is about the experience of living across borders of heart, mind, and heritage.

Liminal Lit exists to create a space for writing that resists simplification. We believe writers should not need to reduce their identities to be understood.

Issue III: Return

Theme: Return

Return explores what comes back and what it means to encounter it again. We invite work that considers return in all its forms, whether chosen or unwanted, joyful or painful, literal or imagined.

What changes when we revisit something we thought we had left behind—and what happens when neither we nor what we return to is quite the same?

Submission Information

Frequently Asked Questions

Answers to common questions about submitting and publishing with Liminal Lit.

Do you accept AI-generated work?

No. Liminal Lit does not accept work written or substantially generated by artificial intelligence. Standard spelling and grammar tools are permitted, but the creative work itself must be the writer’s own.

Who owns the copyright to my work?

Authors retain full copyright to their work. If a submission is accepted, Liminal Lit receives First Electronic Publication Rights, which are exclusive for the first 30 days following publication. After that period, those rights become non-exclusive.

Liminal Lit retains the ongoing non-exclusive right to display and archive the work as part of the magazine’s permanent publication record. Complete terms are provided in the publication agreement sent to accepted contributors.

Do you accept simultaneous submissions?

Yes. Please indicate on the submission form that your work is under consideration elsewhere. If the work is accepted or published by another publication while it is under review with Liminal Lit, please email us as soon as possible so we can withdraw it from consideration.

Do you accept previously published work?

No. We consider previously unpublished work only. This includes work that has been published in print or made publicly available online in full.

Can I submit to more than one genre?

Yes. Writers may submit in a maximum of two genres during each reading period. Both genre submissions must be included in the same submission form response.

How many times may I complete the submission form?

Each writer may complete the submission form only once per issue. Please make sure all materials, including submissions in up to two genres, are included before sending the form.

Is there a submission fee?

No. Submission to Liminal Lit is always free.

Are contributors paid?

Yes. Every contributor published in Issue II: After receives a $100 honorarium.

When will I hear back?

An estimated response date will appear after you submit the form. Our usual response time is approximately two to four weeks after the reading period closes.

Can I withdraw my submission?

Yes. To withdraw a submission, please email us as soon as possible with your name, genre, and the title of the work you would like to withdraw.

Can I submit again if my work is not accepted?

Yes. We welcome returning writers and encourage you to submit again during a future reading period. However, each writer may complete the submission form only once for the current issue.

Can I resubmit a piece that was previously declined?

Yes, as long as the piece has undergone substantial revision since its previous submission. Please do not resubmit an identical version of work that Liminal Lit has previously declined.

Still have a question?

If your question is not answered here, please contact us at liminallit@gmail.com.

Editor’s Note

Some of the most meaningful stories are written in moments of transition, between languages, homes, identities, beliefs, and versions of ourselves. When we announced our first issue, I hoped to gather voices willing to inhabit those spaces with honesty.

Every submission answered Between differently. Some explored memory, migration, family, grief, faith, or love, yet together they revealed the quiet beauty of life lived in the in-between.

Thank you to every writer who entrusted us with their work, and to every reader joining us for the beginning of Liminal Lit. I hope these pages stay with you long after you have turned the final page.

Sophie Baryalai

A Note from the Editor

Dear Readers,

When I founded Liminal Lit, I hoped to create a home for writers across cultures, generations, and experiences, regardless of background or recognition.

For After, we received over 800 submissions, nearly six times that of our inaugural issue. As editor-in-chief, I read every submission myself, discovering perspectives that reshaped my understanding of what after could mean. When I chose this theme, I expected After to center on culture and what follows migration and displacement. Instead, the writers showed me how culture lives alongside so many other afters: grief, family, marriage, sexuality, and the quiet ways we become someone new.

To every writer who entrusted us with their words, thank you. I’m deeply grateful to our contributors and to the growing community that makes Liminal Lit possible.

With gratitude,

Sophie Baryalai

What Remains After

by Chiwenite Onyekwelu

✦
In Cathedral Basilica, a bronze statue of Mary stands,
her hands open, like whorls, a child seated
        on her laps. Our Lady who glistens in the dark,
        I have come to you in search of light.
No one prays for a miracle with clear eyes.
I’d just stepped out of the hospital
        where a woman I love was sliced open.
        They tore her breasts off. I believe
in mastectomy as I believe in apparitions as I believe
in that account of how you appeared
        in France just to bring roses to a girl.
        Notre-Dame de Lourdes. Keep safe my aunt
flattened under a surgeon’s hand,
her necessary wound
        which must now stand in place of breasts.
        Once, Virgin Mary, you taught saint Juan Diego
everything he knew about grace. I want this: a miracle
despite my disbelief, a miracle because of her
        disbelief. Pour inside her skin
        your myrrh. Grant her the tranquillity
of an eye that has seen your crown, and if you must leave
something behind, let it be life.
✦

My Father’s Hand

by Chiwenite Onyekwelu

✦

After Sage Ravenwood’s “Bookends”

Here the hand that burrows deep,
a son's bruised face.
Here a man’s violence leathered
         thin. What other evidence
than these scars, my refusal to walk
back home. Some days
I awake in a distant land, my eyes
                 tender and full of tears.
There are days the hand returns, old
& quivering— its wireless
attempts to hold me close. Between
                  us there are eight cities,
familiar paths more rust than bridge.
When he says I love you,
it is the first time in twenty three years,
                   so I jerk the phone away
from my ear, away because reflex
is the body’s way of revealing
shock. Times like this, I yearn deeply
              for the years before the hand:
each season of corn, those cold nights
around a fireside
& his blanket afterward, even his arms
               spread out as I ran homewards
from school. Love was always there
when no one looked,
& the hand, quivering, points me to it—
                  Here, it’s asking me to see
beyond the clenched fist, to separate
        the man I knew from the thing I didn’t.
✦

Into the Luminous

by Chiwenite Onyekwelu

✦
Now that I have come to the end
of pain, let me suckle, like Adam,
the wet juice of joy.
Bend to the ground,
feel the ginger’s heart
and the rhubarb’s pulse.
Listen, in silence, to my slow rhythmic
stride. The wreck is over and we must go
toward the after-ruin,
toward the quiet
trembling of music, the soft nudge
of an open door. Keep your palm
where once you kept my pain,
unstrap my neck
from this anchored rein. I watched him
die. Tended his heart as I would a field.
He groaned and clenched,
drowned in the blood filling up
his chest. Now I must go away
from that mighty plunge.
Wear my joy-face, crack
a joke that doesn’t peel open
my wound. I want to talk about Ariadne,
Bran the Blessed, about saint Fillan who,
trapped in the dark,
lit up a cave by drawing light
from his bone. Come. Push me
toward a sunlit cliff,
a fluorescent sheaf, into the reddish glow
of a neon tube. I want to remember
nothing about my father’s body
in a casket, the pale-brown of his face
and his sunken eyes.
Bring me a good wine.
Bring me your clean palm. Ride me
like a horse you placed your bet,
and do not mistake my neighing
for a cry.

Sobhaneh

by Torrey Francis Malek

✦
Eating alone, wishing for the strength
to split a pomegranate
like a phonebook in my palms,
I sense the shift:
three eggs spun in olive oil,
salted, peppered, cooling
into my grandfather’s childhood table,
soft radish, soaked walnut hearts,
red onion cloves, feta jiggling
in its skim of milk,
Barbary bread toasted
through its sesame seeds.
I would trade this coffee
for cardamom tea,
delicious sacrilege,
the communion of a sugar cube
tucked under my tongue,
melting into sweet heat,
into the kind words of taroof
I never learned to offer.
How many cousins have I offended,
the half-breed at the table?
Maza was a maze for measure:
how often had they begun
an old family legend, then slipped
into the comfortable giveh
of their native Farsi,
until I stopped them
with a smile
and the wall of my hand:
No more music. No more music.
I love the sound
but never learned the words
of (y)our world.
Three eggs grow cold.
The coffee curdles. I take
another bite of white toast
and imagine sarshir
instead of salted butter,
my tongue almost remembering
what its past life
forgot.
✦

The Bones Along the Creek Bed Still Belong to Me

by Torrey Francis Malek

✦
Tomorrow morning,
the bones along the creek bed
will still belong to me.
I have flung this
body from abandoned cotton-mill roofs,
kicked off rusted railings where
safeguards
gave me greater air,
into whorls of coiled coontail

not knowing what to put where.

The Three Body Problem

by Kimmy Chang

✦
My mother often dreamt of the one she aborted
before my father was ready to be a dad. A son,
she would accuse, when my father said he
wanted another. He pictured them running miles
together, moving heavy objects. A useful back,
a longer stride. But I was so small.

cut the two off entirely. Give me one
more body I thought I couldn’t leave,
and I might have called it love.

No formula solved it.
The departed remain strict hypotheticals.
I was the only body left between them,
the center they pulled against.

Crinoids: or, Christmas at My Dead Grandparents’ House (a Freezing Haibun)

by Madeline Phillips

✦
Think of the worst thing that’s ever happened to you. Tuck it inside an oyster shell until it turns into something surprising. My mother describes the tines of a fork, and I hear windchimes. Somewhere nearby, a dog barks. The ceiling’s burled wood grain makes me feel like I’m living inside a tree. Whenever I wake up not knowing where I am, I’m here. I think you’d like it here. It’s quiet. Only the dull hum of Dad’s toy trains running around the living room —the eager click of Mom’s kitchen timer. But also, it’s too cold. It takes twenty-four hours for the thermostat to climb from forty-four degrees to seventy. My mother loves to bake up here, in her red gingerbreadmen apron. Loves the cornflower blue cupboards and the brick wall where the pots and pans hang. Sure, the counter clutters up too fast, but it’s better than her house, where the dishes spill into the shower. She makes popovers with jam and butter every day here. Says she is sorry for not feeding me better when I was little. Sorry she and Dad didn’t know I’d have to pay back my student loans someday. How did they not know? No one read them stories before bedtime when they were little. Outside the window, one large icicle clings to a bush. My mother and I both top off our coffee and walk around with jaws clenched like clamshells, grinding our teeth to sand. It’s quiet. Only my father’s muffled cursing, his lumbering step. He groans like an old house. Like the lean birch trees in the wind on my walk. I sing about ravens, then see one. An unseen brook bickers with itself, just like my mother does. A spider efforts slowly over the snow. If I lift her to a tree, she could weave a web. Survive. My clumsy hands bury her. Maybe God doesn’t mean for anything bad to happen. Maybe Their giant hands make tidal waves and wars whenever They try to help. I top the leaning tree we bought for twenty-nine dollars with a cornhusk and cardboard angel. My mother falls asleep as I cut her hair in the dining room. Who lined up all the Matryoshka dolls on the table? How long has it been since someone touched her with this much attention? It’s quiet —the CD player skips. The fluorescent lights whir whenever I want to think. What this house needs are some good mid-century lamps with warm incandescent bulbs. I find a book of mollusks at the local antique store, but it’s too expensive. It has colorful pages of the starfish I turn into when I get too high, like on your birthday last year. Starfish aren’t mollusks. Why are they in this book? (Before I die, I need to tell someone how to read my writing notes —maybe that someone is you: the story is a landscape in my peripheral vision. The parentheses are possibilities —forks in a fairytale path. Which way, which way?) I don’t know what I’m doing with my life. Michael says that starfish used to have stems, like flowers. And little feet at the end of the stems, to grip onto rocks. They had a different name then, and some version of that creature still exists. Their mouths were in a different place, too. My fear has a mouth, but I don’t know where it is. Whether it feeds a river or a monster. I can’t stop thinking about that spider by the brook. Maybe it’s safer under the snow, like the frogs and salamanders in the iced-over vernal pool. It’s been so long since I’ve seen you. I’m worried that when we meet next, I won’t know what to do with my hands.

The One Who Does the Burying

by Christian Emecheta

✦

Papa, you have not looked at me since I came home.

I do not blame you for it. Three years is a long time to hold a silence, and silence in our family is not an absence. It is a thing we keep. We oil it, wrap it in cloth and pass it from hand to hand the way other families pass a staff or an heirloom. Your father kept a silence against his brother for eleven years over a boundary of cocoyam, and when he finally spoke it was only to say, at the man’s burial, that the cocoyam had never been sweet anyway. You told me that story when I was small, laughing so hard you had to hold the doorframe, and I laughed without understanding, because I did not yet know you were teaching me. Everything you ever told me was teaching. Even the silence you would later give me. Even that.

So I have been patient. I have sat in the corner of the compound near the ube tree, where the rain drips through in one thin line no matter how the branches are cut, and I have watched you move through these days of preparation like a man walking in a river against the current. I have waited for you to turn. In a compound this full, with canopies going up and aunties arriving from six villages, and Uncle Ejikemeuwa’s voice climbing over everything like an elephant, a person can wait a long time in a corner and not be seen. I know this. I am trying not to let it enter me the wrong way.

Let me say what I came home to say, whether you turn or not.

You remember the counting. Of course you remember, you are the one who put it in me. But I will say it anyway, because saying a thing in order is half of keeping it, that is your own proverb.

In Umuachala, when a person dies, the body does not enter the ground on the strength of prayers alone. Prayers are for the church, and we give the church its portion. But before the coffin is lowered, the eldest of the dead person’s bloodline must stand at the head of the grave and count the names. Fourteen generations, spoken without paper, without stumbling, from the newly dead all the way back to Achala himself, who carried fire across the Iyioji river in a clay pot when the water was young and the far bank had no people on it. Name to father to father to father, a rope let down into the dark. If the counting is true, the dead person climbs the rope and is gathered. If the counting fails, if a name is dropped or a generation skipped, the rope breaks, and the dead one is left to wander the bank of the Iyioji, calling to a family that can no longer hear.

That is the inheritance of our house, Papa. Not the land, whatever Uncle Ejike believes, standing there by the gate with his folder of papers like a man holding his own head under his arm. Not the zinc on the roof or the two plots at Agunze junction. The names. The rope.

And you gave the rope to me.

I want the listening people to understand how strange that was, what you did. The counting passes through sons. It has passed through sons since Achala, or so Uncle Ejike says, though I notice the dead do not come back to confirm his history for him. When Mama died bringing Kamsiyochukwu into the world, and Kamsi turned out to be another girl, another daughter in a house the town was already calling finished, Uncle Ejike came to you within the month. I was nine, on the floor behind the water drum, and I heard him tell you, gently, the way a man is gentle when he is taking something, that the counting would have to come to his side now. That his son Obinna would learn the names. That this was not a matter for grief, it was simply how a river flows, downhill, along the channel cut for it.

You listened to him the way you listen, with your chin down and your eyes up. Then you said, and I have kept these words twenty years, you said, A name does not check the mouth that carries it, Ejike. It checks the heart.

He laughed at you. He drank your palm wine, laughed and went home believing he had won, because you did not argue further, and in his understanding, a man who stops arguing has surrendered. He has never learned that in this family, silence is not surrender. Silence is the oiled thing, the wrapped thing.

That night you woke me when the moon was high enough to put shadows under the orange tree, and you took me to the back room where the yam barn used to be, and you taught me the first three names. Ginikanwa, you said, this is not a song. Do not learn it the way you learn songs, up in the mouth where things are light. Put it down low, where you keep the things you would run into a burning house for.

I remember I asked you, half asleep, why my own name came first, since I was not dead. And you said, without smiling, “The counting always begins with the one who will do the burying.” You learn it with yourself at the top so that you never forget the rope is attached to you. One day you will lift your name off the top and put mine there, and the rope will still hold, because you were always part of it.

I did not understand you then. Papa, I am beginning to understand you now, and the understanding is costing me more than I can say.

We went on like that for years, at night, in the dry season and the rains. Dimkpa, son of Ikemba. Ikemba, son of Nwosu. You taught me the pauses along with the names, because the counting is not a race. There is a breath you take between the seventh generation and the eighth, at Obiefuna, who buried three of his own children in one harmattan and still stood at his father’s grave and counted without his voice breaking. You stop there, you told me. You give Obiefuna his silence. A counting without that pause is correct the way a coffin is a box. It holds the thing, but it does not honor it.

Fourteen generations, and beneath them Achala with his pot of fire, and beneath Achala nothing but the river.

And then I left.

I will not pretend the leaving was gentle. You wanted me in Umuachala. You had it arranged in your mind like a room you had already swept, myself in the house, a husband from a counting family, Kamsi growing up under my hand, the names safe in the town where they were grown. And I stood in the doorway with my bag already packed for Agunze, for the typing pool at the ministry and the evening classes, and I said the thing I have carried since like a stone in the shoe.

I said, “Your names are a debt, Papa.” A debt I never agreed to. You are asking me to spend my one life servicing what dead men borrowed.

You did not shout. I think I wanted you to shout. You looked at me for a long moment, and then you picked up your cutlass and went out to the back as though I had told you the new price of rice, and that was the beginning of the silence, our silence, the one we oiled and wrapped and kept between us for three years. I called twice the first year. Kamsi answered both times, whispering like a smuggler, telling me you were in the yard, you were at a meeting, you were asleep. The second year I stopped calling and told myself it was pride. The third year I stopped telling myself anything at all. The city is good at that. It lets you set a thing down and walk away from it, and it never once reminds you that the thing is still sitting where you left it, still yours, waiting with the patience of the dead.

Then, two weeks ago, came Kamsi’s message. Papa is not well. His chest. He will not go to Agunze for tests, he says what is checking him is not in Agunze. Ginika, come home.

So I came home. I took the night bus because it was cheaper and because I could not bear one more day of preparing myself. The bus smelled of dried fish and engine heat. A woman beside me ate groundnuts one at a time, slowly, as if each one had to be persuaded. The rain started somewhere after the junction, the driver leaning into the windscreen, the road turning its shoulder against us at the long bend below Ugwu Ntamu, where the Iyioji swings in close and runs beside the road like a black dog keeping pace.

I remember the water.

I do not want to talk about the water yet.

What I want to talk about is this compound, and these days, and the things I have watched since I arrived, because Papa, something in this house has gone wrong, and I need you to hear me the way you have never quite heard me, not as a daughter making noise but as the one you gave the rope to.

Number one. Nobody in this compound will say my name. Not Mama Nkechi, ladling egusi under the canopy for forty people, calling every child and cousin by name as she serves them, setting out plates until the table has no more room, and setting none for me. Not the aunties from Umudim, who talk about me the way people talk about a season that has passed. She was stubborn, that one, but the stubbornness came from the father. They speak of me and around me and through me, and my name never once crosses their teeth. At first, I told myself it was the old anger, the town’s long memory for a daughter who left. But Papa, even anger says a name. Anger loves a name. This is something else. This is a house holding its mouth the way you hold a lamp in wind.

Number two. My bag is still by the door of the main room, exactly where it was put on the first day, and you will not let anyone touch it. Aunty Mgbafor tried to move it to sweep and you came across that room so fast, an unwell man, a man with an aching chest, and you took it from her hands without one word and set it back in its place and stood over it, breathing. I watched Aunty Mgbafor look at you and decide, in the way of women who have buried people, not to speak. The bag has not been opened. It is water stained, my bag. The zip has a scarf of river weed caught in it, dried now, pale as old thread. I keep meaning to clean it and I keep not going near it.

Number three. The dogs. Ozoemena’s dogs, that used to climb me like a tree when I was a girl, stand at the far fence when I pass and bark with their hackles up, then whine, then bark again, as if I am something they remember and cannot place. The fowls will not cross my path. A hen will change her whole journey, gather her chicks and go around the long way by the kitchen, rather than walk the line I have walked.

Number four, and Papa, this is the one that sits on my chest at night. The grave.

The men came on Thursday with their shovels and their tape, six of them, Ejike directing as though digging were a thing that could be supervised into excellence. I stood at the edge of the family plot behind the orange tree, where Mama lies, and Grandmother Odoziaku. I watched them measure, and I saw at once that the measurement was wrong. Papa, you are a tall man. Tall enough that the church in Umuachala built its new doorway with you in mind, the carpenter said so at the dedication and the whole town laughed. And the grave they measured was short. Neat, well cut, its corners squared the way Ejike likes things, and short. I said it aloud. I said, that is not the right measurement, and the men went on digging in the thin rain, the kind that does not fall so much as gather on you, and not one of them lifted his head.

Nobody in this compound has heard me since I came home.

There. I have said it now, the one that troubles me most. I have been talking since I arrived, Papa. To Mama Nkechi at her pots. To Kamsi, my Kamsi, thirteen now, long legged and holding her grief with both hands like a bowl too full to walk with. I sat beside her on the back step and talked until my talking felt like the wind in the ube tree, present, continuous, unremarked. She looked through me once, straight through the middle of me, at the fence beyond, her face folded, and she got up and went inside. And my hem, Papa. I want you to look at my hem. Since the night bus, since the bend below Ugwu Ntamu, my hem has not dried. Everything else about me is as it was, my hands, my headscarf, my two lives of memory, but the bottom of my dress stays dark and heavy with moisture, and I no longer let myself wonder why.

Yesterday was the counting.

The compound filled early. They put your good chair under the mango, the one with the carved arms, and led you to it, one man at each elbow, and I saw what these three years and these two weeks have done to your body. I am sorry in the old full way, the way that has no use for the word sorry. Beside the open grave they set the coffin on two benches, the coffin was short, and on top of it someone had placed a framed photograph, but from my corner by the ube tree I could not see whose face was in it, and I did not go closer. I want to tell you it was because of the crowd.

Uncle Ejike stood up to count.

He had dressed for it. New wrapper, the red cap he has been rehearsing with for years. He set his folder of land papers on the seat behind him, first things first, raised his hands for quiet, and began. Son of Okwudili. Son of Dimkpa. Son of Ikemba. Son of Nwosu. Son of Chukwuka. The compound held itself still. Even the fowls had gone to the far fence, over by the dogs.

And then he reached the place between the seventh generation and the eighth. He reached Obiefuna.

And he did not pause.

He went straight through, son of Obiefuna, son of Ezenwa, at speed like a man reading a list of goods off a lorry, and the counting was correct the way a coffin is a box, and I felt the wrongness of it travel through the gathered people like cold through water, though not one of them could have told you what was missing, only that something was. Only you knew. I watched it reach you. I watched your hands close on the carved arms of the chair.

You stood up.

The two men moved to help you and you put them aside with a look. You walked to the head of the grave, past your brother, who stopped counting with Ezenwa half out of his mouth, you stood where the earth was open, and you were tall, Papa, you were tall the way the church doorway remembers. You did not continue the rope from where Ejike had dropped it. You did not correct him or take up the eighth generation. You lifted your face, and into the silence of the whole compound you said one name. The first name. The name that goes at the top.

You said, Ginikanwa.

You said, Ginikanwa, daughter of Nwabueze.

Papa.

Papa, the water was so fast. That is the part I could not say until now. The bend below Ugwu Ntamu, the road’s wet shoulder, the bus going over slowly, almost politely, the way large things fall. The window would not take my fist and then it took my fist, and the Iyioji came in wearing all its cold, and I thought, in the small bright center of that dark, I have not put his name at the top. I have not done the one thing. The rope is attached to me and I am in the river.

The counting always begins with the one who will do the burying. You taught me that. You never told me what it means when the order breaks. When the rope is let down for the daughter by the father, when the one who learned the names is the one climbing.

But you stood at my grave, at my short and well squared grave, and you counted. Ginikanwa, daughter of Nwabueze. Nwabueze, son of Okwudili. Okwudili, son of Dimkpa. And at the place between the seventh and the eighth, at Obiefuna who buried three of his own children in one harmattan, you stopped. You gave him his silence. You gave him a long silence, Papa, and I understood that it was no longer only his. Your voice went down into the pause and gathered there and came up again carrying Obiefuna’s dead children and carrying me, and you finished the rope, all fourteen, down to Achala and his pot of fire, and the coffin went into the ground with my photograph looking at the sky, and I felt the rope hold. I felt it take my weight. I am telling you so you will know. It held.

I have one more thing to do before I climb.

Last night I went to Kamsi where she slept with her knees drawn up, and I lay down beside my sister the way I used to when she was small and the rain frightened her, and I put my mouth near her ear, and I began. Not as a song. Down low, where the things are kept that a person would run into a burning house for. Kamsiyochukwu, daughter of Nwabueze. Nwabueze, son of Okwudili. She stirred. Her lips moved. Papa, I stayed until first light, and I gave her the whole rope, the names and the pauses, the breath at Obiefuna, everything you put into me, and when Mama Nkechi’s pots began in the kitchen, Kamsi sat up in the grey light with her eyes still full of sleep and said, out loud, to no one the aunties could see, Okwudili, son of Dimkpa. Then she looked at the corner where I was standing. Papa, she looked at me, not through. Only for a moment. It was enough.

So you will not need to search for me. There is no hole in any fence. When the last mourners go and the canopies leave, you may feel the house grow lighter by a certain weight, and that will be me, gone the way I came, along the road that runs beside the river. Do not let Ejike near her learning. Do not let anyone tell her a name checks the mouth that carries it. And when your own day comes, Papa, and I am asking the river to keep that day far from now, my Kamsi will stand at the head of your grave, a daughter counting for her father in Umuachala, and she will lift her own name off the top and set yours there, and the rope will hold, because she was always part of it.

I was angry when I called the names a debt. I was young, and the young think a debt is only what is taken from you. Nobody tells you that some debts are how the dead keep loving you, payment after payment, mouth after mouth, all the way down to the water.

Look at her, Papa, when she counts. She carries me now.

The counting does not end with me.

The Last to Eat

by Adam Porkolab

✦

I came home in October, the month the orchard ripens. The bus dropped me at the foot of the village, and I walked the road my grandmother had walked for sixty-eight years, and the houses were dark, and no light came on behind me as I passed. The smell met me before the first gate: apples on the ground, soft, fermenting, the brown sweetness already going over to vinegar. I had told myself I was coming to clear the fruit, empty the house, and give the keys to an estate agent in town before winter.

My grandmother was waiting in the kitchen, but only the way the dead wait. She had laid herself out properly: hands folded, mouth held closed by a strip of linen, a green apple set beside her on the cloth. The knot was tucked under her left ear, where she could have reached it herself. The apple was hard and unbitten. It was for me. I put it in my pocket and went out the kitchen door.

The orchard ran from the back of the house down the slope to the stream, and the trees were the family. This is what my grandmother had taught me in the kert when I was four, when she set my palm against the bark of her mother’s tree and told me: This is your great-grandmother. She is sweet in late September. Eat her slowly. Each tree of ours had been planted from a bone — a tooth or a knuckle, sometimes a piece of skull — withheld from the burial and pressed into the soil under a sapling. The body went to the ground whole. The bone went to the tree. The apple came to the mouth. That was the round. To eat one of our dead in the autumn was to carry a sliver of their voice in the body until spring, when the tree took the voice back and the round began again.

I had been gone twelve years.

I stood at the top of the orchard and counted. Forty-six trees from the stream up the slope, and at the edge where the ground flattens, a forty-seventh: thin, but not new. The soil had settled around it. The scar where its first apple had hung was already hard and brown. My grandmother had planted herself before summer, before the last two letters I had not answered. She had walked out one morning with a tooth in her palm, pressed it into the earth, and gone home to wait. The tree had risen the way trees rise here. Its one apple was in my pocket.

The other trees were heavy. The apples had not been picked in years. They lay on the ground in black mash, and where they hung they were rotting, full of wasps. I put my palm against my great-grandmother’s bark. I had come to clear the fruit. That was still what I called it. My grandmother had eaten what she could, but an old woman cannot eat an orchard. Each unpicked apple was an ancestor with no mouth left to receive it. They were inside the trees still.

I went to the oldest, by the stream, and I knelt, and I took an apple from the lowest branch, and I ate.

Sarjú I was cutting when the Russians came, a man said in a Palóc so old the consonants had moved, and half of him I lost, and I tasted bread and the inside of a stable. I was seven when the fever came and stayed, a boy said, and I tasted iron and chamomile. I washed my husband’s body and could not weep, a woman said, and the fruit went to lye and starch in my mouth. They burned the mill in the spring of eighteen forty-eight, a miller said, and I tasted ash on rye.

I worked along the row, eating one, two, sometimes four to clear a tree that had held a heavy life. By the twelfth my gums were bleeding. My grandfather laughed at me from his tree because I was eating wrong, biting toward the stem. By twenty my jaw clicked each time I bit. I vomited behind my grandfather’s trunk, brown seeds and peel in the grass while two dead men argued behind my teeth; then I wiped my mouth and picked the next apple. My great-aunt, who had drowned in the stream, was patient and gave me her whole crop in three apples and was gone. A wasp stung the roof of my mouth at the thirty-eighth tree. I swallowed it with the fruit. Some of them I did not know. A man whose name was not in any letter I had ever read called me by my grandmother’s name. The last five trees I ate standing because I was afraid I would not get up again. Forty-six trees. The wasps had gone slow with the cold. Dusk came with my hands shaking and a mound of cores darkening at my feet.

I came at last to the sapling. There was no apple on it. I sat down with my back against the thin bark, took the apple from my pocket, and ate.

My grandmother was small inside the apple, and what came was not sentences. A place, she said. Ready. Beside me.

There was. I could see it. A cleared patch of ground next to her sapling, the soil broken and waiting, long enough for a man. I leaned over and put my hand into the soil. It was already warm.

I lay down in it. The earth took the shape of my shoulders and hips. I kept one hand buried beside my face while the orchard moved in my mouth. I stayed until the warmth under my back began to feel like permission. But there was no one left to plant me. There was no one left to come back in twelve years and find my apple and eat it. To be planted in the kert you needed a mouth to come after, and I was the last mouth. Then I rolled onto my side and pushed myself out. My palms came away black.

The orchard was lighter now. They carried me back up the slope from inside. In the kitchen my grandmother was where I had left her, hands folded, linen holding her mouth shut. The spade leaned behind the door. I stood beside her with her taste still in my mouth, the roof of it swollen around the sting. The body went to the ground whole. I pulled the cloth up over her face and left her on the table.

The bus would not come until morning. I would die in the city, eventually, in the rented flat where I buy apples in a net bag from the shop on the corner, and the forty-seven of them would go into a leased plot, and in twenty-five years somebody would dig them up to make room. But tonight I would sit at the side of the road, and I would feel them turning over in me, and I would not be alone.

Not yet.

Derrida Ain’t Answering

A Scandinavian family drama

by Tytti Heikkinen

✦

You, or you, i.e., I, can make innocent but irreversible mistakes, like being too tired to get outta bed to throw a piece of chewing gum away, so the next day, when you, i.e., I, try to peel it off the book cover, it’s stuck there like shit on a country bumpkin’s cottage wall, as they say in Dad’s childhood family, which family he doesn’t fancy a lot, as he grew too academic for that. I like them, though. Technically, the gum could be removed from the book just like Dad removed himself from his family, but here comes the brain teaser: rip off the whole freaking lump and ruin the cover, or live with the lump. Which one would you choose? Well, shit.

///

Mom and Dad argue about everything. I don’t get how they ever managed to make a lump of their own, i.e., the Me-mandolin. I mean, the operation must have required at least some level of cooperation, especially if they voluntarily got married. “I” happened because those two were students and really into semantics—and still are, hello?! What’s wrong with people who stick to their youth’s delusions, like, foreva?

I was conceived in the euphoric aftermath of the university occupation. When night fell, all the mop-headed turtleneck groovers and long-haired Chelsea girls in their suede miniskirts (but politically aware miniskirts) put down their pamphlets and megaphones and went to sleep in the freshly occupied hall. It’s just that there were only so many sleeping bags… Then the irreversible happened: after 32 weeks or so, I popped out of the bag, and only a hair premature at that! Literally a hair, as I had fur, or more specifically, the kind of lanugo hair that some premature babies have. It was gone before Mom and Dad could decide which side of the family was to blame for the hairy offspring. I miss my lanugo!

Once these two semanticists no longer had to be ashamed of their hairy offspring, they held a christening and gave it a totally unimaginative name. They also applied for lectureships at the very university they had tried to burn down. Turned out all they had managed to set on fire were their genitals: the rest of the story is the lamest Educated Scandi Dream ever, earning douze points from every country in the Eurovision Song Contest.

///

Mom and Dad bought a “pitsihuvila,” a kind of gingerbread villa, although it was totally against their *values*. They also went to driving school, because it just made sense to worry about global warming and at the same time buy a diesel van to get the renovation stuff to the villa. I so disagree. It’s either/or, not “både och” as they say in Swedish, Mom’s mother tongue. In other words, you, i.e., she, can’t just cherry-pick whatever works best, but that’s exactly what those two did. When I questioned Mom about it, she just went, “Entten-tentten-teelikamentten.” That’s the standard procedure in her family. Entten-tentten-teelikamentten is Swedish, or at least Finnish-Swedish, but the thing is more complicated than that, because it’s just gibberish kids use when they play or want to drive their parents up the wall. So creepy to see how your own parent regresses to that level when morally cornered.

It totally blew Mom’s mind that every language has its own gibberish that only makes sense in that language, so she went on to do research on it. She’s secretly proud of her achievements in a field that doesn’t mean anything. In reality, this villa is the best venture she has ever been involved in.

///

The miniskirts and turtlenecks were buried in the attic with mothballs, and probably Dad’s balls too, since he never set anything on fire anymore, except the backyard grill of course. There he roasts beef in caveman style, only the beef is made of seitan. I wonder what goes through his head as he stares at the flames and turns those seitanic lumps of his.

Dad, the crazy person, starts grilling already in March. When he does, he looks like a member of his childhood family, somehow lost in my and Mom’s backyard. We spy on him from the kitchen. The café curtain sways in the draft, because the window is original to the villa and in desperate need of restoration.

Sometimes I have this rare urge to put my beanie on and go serve as Dad’s faithful assistant. I can’t, because things like that were never on his family’s to-do list. I mean, he just can’t handle any father-daughter stuff. It’s like learning to ride a bike: there’s a period where it’s easy to get the hang of it, and if you miss it, you miss it. It’s a different story with Mom: her family have been ministers and merchants for generations, so they’re socially fluent. It’s just that they carefully avoid actually saying anything, so, basically, it’s the same shite in a different wrapper, as they would say in Dad’s family.

///

What kinda rocket science do you need to unstick one piece of gum from a book?!

///

I wake up to the smell of synthetic peppermint. I so wish chewing gum had never been invented. I wish Derrida would’ve locked in and got the bag, so he could’ve written checks instead of semantics. I wish Montague had written hospital thrillers, and Alfred Tarski had taken his Ritalin, so my parents would NOT! have met.

///

Smell-smell-smell-smell-smell...

///

In Mom’s study, an ancient poster encourages the joys of sober living. There’s a rockabilly boy and a rockabilly girl walking arm-in-arm in black leather biker jackets, which jackets totally don’t suit their cutesiness. Above their heads, which are crusted with a ponytail and an Elvis-like grease tower, there’s loopy cursive that reads: ~*~ Smell it, taste it—summer, without cigarettes, of course. ~*~

Clearly, the poster was designed by some middle-aged gray panthers to appeal to the *younger set*, but everything in it is so godawfully wrong that when I want to hurt myself, I go stare at it, listening to my eyeballs crackle. Mom stares at the poster too, but she sees something else in it, something about youth lost, etc. Too bad, Mom: once you had your gum too, metaphorically speaking, and you slapped it on a jacket, still metaphorically speaking, and then, far too soon, life as you knew it was irrevocably over. It went up in smoke, as the saying goes, or disappeared like a fart in the Sahara, as Dad’s family puts it.

///

There’s no chance I’ll ever get to study it, so I might as well stop reading for the entrance exams, so: ~*~ Smell it, taste it—summer, with weed, of course. ~*~

///

Gum-gum-gum-gum-GUM!

///

Oddly enough, Dad’s favorite theorist is Lacan, the golden boy of a rich bourgeois family, and his philosophy was totally over-the-top convoluted. Mom’s favorite philosopher is Althusser, so stiff and expressionless, likely because he spent his childhood in genuine poverty. When it comes to the Me-mandolin, I’m painfully cognizant that I’m the self-aware offspring of two un-self-aware parents and that my lack of interest in anything is a mandatory yet pathetically rebellious rite of passage. I also know my superficiality is highly artificial and, in hindsight, calculated, but what else can you expect from the synthesis of two tedious semanticists? At least you can expect that one morning it gets an Idea and ascends to the attic, where the air doesn’t smell of peppermint but of the Authenticity of dead mice.

///

Mom’s suede miniskirt gets good reception at the nightclub. It has that air of the exceptional about it, making it cool despite the mothball pong. Too bad it attracts all kinds of attention. Maybe it’s this animalistic smell that lures the other animals. The groping is infuriating beyond belief. OK, clearly it’s easy to put a hand on mon cul, but does that really mean one should? Don’t people understand the concept of decency anymore? Hello, this is a MOTHER’S skirt! Hands are all over me, and I just stare at the pulsating dance floor. I want to sleep in a bag and come out of it like, neva. Negative aura.

///

I get back to the villa at dawn. Don’t want to lie next to the gum, so I go to the parlor, my favorite part of the house. The air smells faintly of fresh paint. Mom and Dad painted the floor traditional gray after trying all the other alternatives. I kinda like how calm and even those thick layers of trial and error feel against my feet. The chill of the floor gives way to the softer chill of the rag rugs. After a wash in the lake, they smell of pine soap. The walls are yellowish white, like piss but in an uplifting way. The height makes the parlor churchlike, and for a reason: these kinds of rooms were meant to make everybody feel small. Dad has a habit of studying the ceiling, his arms covered in goosebumps, as if he was, I dunno, hunting down the white moose, the Scandi version of Moby Dick in his mythology article.

The dawn sun penetrates the old, wavy glass of the agonizingly beautiful arched windows. The light colors the room a rosy pink. I guess that in a place like this, people should be happier than we are. Mom and Dad aren’t happy anywhere; they’re too busy searching for Real Happiness™. Would they be more relaxed if they had been real old money folks with an inherited villa? Philosophy never gave them answers to anything, and they’re goofy enough to take that as the answer.

The piano has stood silent for ages even though Mom stubbornly keeps putting the sheet music on top. The notes gather dust in the silk folders, bluer than the night. I know: I watch those folders the way Mom watches her poster. Could I ever play again? Could Mom begin her youth again? I could wake those two by beating the keys, but do I want to? I could want to. I could beat the keys for so long that eventually the sound would turn into music. The problem is you cannot wake them up. They could have beat the keys too, but they didn’t. They got the careers they claim they don’t want (they do), and the money they claim they don’t need (they do). If those weren’t the goals, why did they try so eagerly to achieve them? This room is so calm and airy and silent.

///

In the very beginning, I was grown in an incubator. The doctors kept shoving instruments there, prodding and poking my hairy back. I kept spinning like seitan on a grill. Because of the infection risk, even Mom and Dad could touch me only through plastic sleeves that hung from the incubator’s side like fingered snouts. For the longest time, that was the only way we could connect.

Undoubtedly, the time in the incu-pod shaped my understanding of the parent-child bond. Perhaps Mom and Dad realize that and know not to come too close. It also shaped my sense of space. No wonder I like the parlor. I need a place where the air will never run out again and where I can’t hit the ceiling just by raising my hand.

///

I’ve honed my skillset at dodging gropers in the nightclub. An unexpected side effect: I’ve managed to improve my dancing considerably, or at least that’s what the majority says, at least when I force them to answer. So, all in all, I’d call the night a double jackpot, maybe even triple. Namely...

One guy asked me whether I understood the problem with wearing a look-at-me skirt and then getting pissed off when someone actually took the bait. I could have answered “entten-tentten-teelikamentten.” I didn’t, because it would totally have destroyed my belief in humankind. Instead, I said “yes.” Then I raised my hands to make the skirt rise even higher and danced away, backwards like a crab with my claw-hands as castanets above my head. I kept looking the guy in the eyes, half-hoping he would throw his glass on the floor and say “that’s it,” and follow me to the end of the world. He didn’t, if only because he had only a can of soda and thus was pathetically sober. I liked it; his unexpected decency lured me in. I immediately knew he was the legal guardian of his orphaned siblings; he had that responsible, adult-too-soon vibe written all over his face. So, I guess I’m obliged to talk to him if he ever pops up in the club again. I kinda wish he would.

///

Every summer, the local community puts on a fair. Despite the nightclub night, I drag myself along behind my folks. I nurse my hangover, wondering: when the inevitable hits, is there a trash bin I could vomit into, or, as they say in Dad’s family, speak Danish into? I’m already gagging.

“Look at that,” Dad says.

“What are they?” Mom asks, enchanted.

Haven’t these two ever seen air dancers?! Maybe not. They never go anywhere. They’d rather stay home and quarrel about the death of postmodernism. We’re not the only gawkers; we’re joined by a group of under-sevens and a drunk. The kids are too adorable, and I guess the drunk is too, in her “I could have had it all, but something came my way” way. Something also came the way of Dad’s oldest brother Jan-Erik, making him the official chief drunk among the drunks in their family tree. Dad is ashamed of Jan-Erik, maybe even more because he would have wanted an older brother he could worship. At least I would have liked one. I reckon Dad shouldn’t be so mad at Jan-Erik. I mean, everybody is good at something, and Jan-Erik is good at boozing. Everybody drinks anyway, right, and then they just hold back their vomit, because that’s how it goes. That’s life, and right now, it’s my life. It’s a vibe.

“I’ll go check the book stalls,” I manage to say before the next vomit wave hits. Then I escape the scene of the almost-crime before Dad and Mom have time to say anything.

///

I kinda hope to find a gum-free version of my book, but these stalls only carry the most generic book-club stuff. Correction: one does have a biography of Jean Genet and a book on the global water crisis, not bad. It’s just that these are real books, so they come with real prices. So, it’s either the books or yet another nightclub night with “witamin C.” Gods of wisdom, what will it be? This is how geniuses solve the equation: they go to the lottery stall, to double the cash and have it all!

“What did you find, hon?” Mom asks. Somehow, she always manages to creep up behind me, now massaging my shoulders. The warm weather has made the age spots on her hands visible. “Oh, that’s good,” she says about the water crisis, hiding her hands behind her back. “Shall I buy it for you? And the other one, too? Something else, maybe?”

If I said yes, I’d get everything without touching the lottery.

“No, that’s OK, I’ll pay for them myself,” I reply. So, goodbye witamins and hello glass of vater. Apparently, I’m no genius; can’t even maximize the benefit.

Mom looks disappointed. Clearly, she would have wanted to give something to the daughter she hardly knows. When she’s in that mode, she always buys me stuff. I already know that this habit of hers will work in my favor in the future. I don’t want to hurt her feelings every second of my life, so I say, “But I could use some ice cream.”

“Now that’s real talk,” Dad jumps in. In this sunlight, his almost black beard looks tinged with orange. Someday it will be gray.

“Ice-cream parlor ice cream?” I ask with my most childlike voice.

“Let’s not get carried away,” Mom says, glancing at Dad. Mom and I know that Dad can’t splurge on anything, not even a kitchen roll. Why spend money on it, when you can use toilet paper instead? Dad could use it as an exam question.

Sometimes miracles do happen. “Come on,” Dad says to Mom, “a couple of ice creams won’t hurt anybody.”

“Well, this once,” Mom says, because today it’s her turn to give in, but the attitude she doesn’t have, because it is Dad’s attitude. These two pervs just don’t make any sense.

We march side by side toward the ice-cream parlor in the park that is almost touchingly old-fashioned. Lilacs and bird cherries smell wonderful, the gravel makes a nerve-soothing sound under our shoes, and finally I stop seeing migraine auras. This is the Disney version of us, walking arm-in-arm, I, the Me-mandolin, in the middle. Even our shadow looks like a real family, in the sense of a true lifestyle magazine family. Alternatively, two prison guards drag their convict by the armpits. Behind our backs, the air dancers bow at the mercy of their internal fans and external forces. Which ones shape their destiny more? I ask Derrida, but he ain’t answering.

///

At home, I put the books on the shelf like, I dunno, trophies. I’m not going to read them. I just collect them for my future me, who will inherit this place and in her turn will pretend it’s nothing, plain nothing.

Someday Mom and Dad will “gå bort,” as Mom’s family puts it, or throw the spoon into the corner, as Dad’s family says. Finally, I will die too. For that reason alone, I need a baby. I can’t stand the thought of the villa ending up at the mercy of property sharks. I’m pretty sure I already feel the urge to have my own hairy-back. So, I guess I need to talk to that guy. Which means I also need a sleeping bag. They cost money, not to mention this place. It isn’t too hard to see why principles tend to fly out the window. Not hard either to see why Mom goes out for a smoke when she thinks Dad doesn’t notice. Dad does the same. They do that so systematically that it’s as if they had managed to telepathically arrange shifts that never overlap. I watch them from my dormer window. Down there, underneath me, one or the other is leaning against the wall, staring at the glowing tip of the cigarette and then the villa’s wooden surface. Mom smears her butt elegantly against her loafer’s heel; Dad snaps his butt to the ground and stomps on it with his rubber boot. Both bury their butts in the expanded clay that they have spread around the villa as if they were building a rampart around a castle. I used to dig the butts out, eager to learn.

“Lika barn leka bäst.”

Similar children play best. The doctors and priests in Mom’s family say so, and although they’re talking about something completely different, they all look at Dad. They do it because they know how to turn passive aggression into an art form. Jan-Erik, in particular, makes them do it. Jan-Erik, in turn, knows how to do something they don’t: he knows how to be devilishly funny. Even Dad doesn’t know how to be like that, although sometimes he seems as if he wishes he did. He has the same fate as the Little Mermaid, who sacrificed her voice to get the prince. Mom sacrificed her voice by turning herself into an overkind goofy person, whose only inherited trait is entten-tentten-teelikamentten.

Sometimes Dad looks as if he wishes Mom would show at least some of the same dominance as the rest of her family and use it against forces outside the family, like the bank and, especially, her own relatives. Mom looks at Dad and then at her poster, as if something in him had once promised her a different kind of life, perhaps even the same kind of madness Jan-Erik has. They found each other in a no-man’s-land offered by the university, but only on the condition that it turned them into the same no-man’s-land. Would they have been happier if they’d known how to play with children like themselves instead of each other? But then again, they were never looking for happiness.

And here it comes, the question which hits me in these kinds of *soul moments*, when I’m hungover: would Mom and Dad be happier without me? I will never find out; the incu-pod will remain between us, like, foreva. If I asked them, they’d both just run out for a smoke, perhaps finally bumping into each other like real adults.

Nobody can get an honest answer if the honest answer does not happen to be the Disney version. No wonder I hate semantics. Words produce lies, even when all the right words are in the right order. Math gives false positives only if you can’t do the math. I have ten fingers, meaning I can.

The only book I want to read has gum on its cover. The gum looks like a small rodent has left its brain behind. I should really start to study, but how, if I can’t even solve this ONE simple problem!! Would somebody care to help? Althusser? Lacan? Anybody? Nobody, so I take the book and start painfully slogging through mini lie after mini lie while palpating a lump of dried gum and saliva beneath my fingers.

Recalculating

by Laila Halaby

✦

Wednesday morning.

Demi settles into a window seat at the back where she can be lulled by engine noise and wafts of diesel. Her back crick-creaks up and down the hard, plastic seat. A few breaths and her blood slows to a putter after the running, climbing, and finding her seat. She shouldn’t be this tired or creaky, but here she is. A young man wrapped in cologne and cigarettes sits down next to her. His body fills his seat but does not creep into hers. His gaze is forward and they do not acknowledge one another.

The gray noise of the bus’s rumble-bump often succeeds in quieting the busy inside her, urban White noise with the power to erase everything else. She giggles out loud at the thought of a giant White eraser. She feels the young man next to her turn his head. He is not White so he might understand, might even laugh with her. He is also a man, so he might just think she is crazy. She closes her eyes.

The bus stops for the ups and downs of people and their things. The young man beside her does not move. She is never scared to sit next to a young man on the back of a city bus; if anything, it comforts her. When her mind begins its sneaky run around corners and back alleys, she pops her eyes open. The back of a man’s head with tough gray hairs pushing themselves out between the brown. His white shirt collar is yellowing at the crease.

The bus heaves forward. Hum and stop, hum and stop. Two people get off. Two people get on. A fair exchange. The bus will now ride for almost a mile without stopping as it goes under the highway. It begins its lurch forward and her gaze lands on the second of the newest passengers, a handsome young man dressed in a long shiny white dress and a fur coat. She smiles at him, this angel of a man, and he smiles back.

Recalculating.

She feels the man next to her looking at her. When she turns, she sees he is looking past her out the window and in that same instant she feels his body come alive and hears the cry from deep within him.

She follows his gaze out the window, across the wide stretch that separates east and westbound traffic, and onto the road beyond that is littered with smoke and small heaps and two cars flipped upside down and frantic movement and rain.

When had it begun to rain?

The young man to her right mutters words in a language she can’t identify. He is half-standing and leaning over her. He is wearing heavy boots and she hopes that he will not step on her feet. She stares out the window and feels nothing as she watches the people run toward the cars and the heaps. There is an escalating clamor of people’s voices on the bus as domino-like they become aware of the horrors going east, gasping and screaming and calling out to the bus driver to stop.

What are you going to do about it? Demi wants to shout. What could you possibly do from way over here?

Oblivious, the bus continues its path forward and the heads of the passengers crane back to watch.

“The two cars must have flipped off the highway to the road below,” the angel-man in the fur coat says in a voice that carries back to Demi, who is both horrified and relieved by the inability to do anything besides witness.

The bus follows the curve of the road and gradually everyone returns their collective gaze forward.

The beautiful angel-man in the dress and fur coat is no longer standing.

The man next to her is sitting again. “Awful,” he says.

“Horrible,” says Demi, whispering prayer words and looking down toward the ground out of respect for the tragedy, which is a knife that has slit open everyone’s reserve. The bus is filled with a new hum as the no-longer strangers talk to one another. The young man next to her clenches and unclenches his hands on his thighs and is rocking gently forward and back. Demi puts her hand on his arm and they each whisper words in their own language.

*

Four years ago, she and her husband and her trying-to-be grown son had gone on a desperate holiday and acquainted themselves with Ireland. Her husband had mapped out each town and they had stopped frequently for picnics and wanders in meadows, and for castles and pubs. Their son was in charge of the GPS and whenever they deviated from the intended route, something they did often, a woman’s voice would tell them she was recalculating. In the years that followed, recalculating became their word to tell the others they were getting back on track. It turned out to be everyone’s word to tell people they were getting back on track, so they spoke it with an Irish accent to make it theirs.

Not long after their return, her son had begun – or continued – to use drugs in earnest. He would be her sweet boy one moment and then a violent stranger the next. He lied as he breathed and routinely stole from them. He hocked their two televisions and her gold. When he took the entire sound system that her husband had spent days installing and cherished over all his possessions, he had threatened to kick him out.

They all fought. Demi’s husband blamed her for spoiling him. Demi blamed her husband for being too tough with him.

She and her son moved out.

Her son carried suitcases of apologies and promises of rehab and sobriety to their new apartment. As tough as she thought she was, without the presence of her husband her son had no internal policing. She often came home from work to find broken and scary people filling the tiny space of her new home and when she told them to leave, her son threatened her. On two occasions she chased him and his friends away with the metal baseball bat that she had found next to a dumpster and had learned to always keep near her when she was home. A few sleepless days would pass and her son would come back and apologize, promising it would never happen again. She would always pretend to believe him.

He pinched a ruby ring given to her a lifetime ago by her now dead uncle that was the last of the valuable jewelry and that she had hidden inside her underwear drawer. He even stole her gold chain with the charms that she wore every day but removed at night.

It was like a crash course in loss – how to live without any of the things that matter to you or that you think make you whole, and then to lose more.

*

Images of her son still come to her at first waking and replay throughout the day until those last moments in the evening when she is fighting sleep so that she won’t wake in the middle of the night thinking he’ll be there and realize he will never be there again, which sends her banging around her empty apartment. Better to fuddle awake being slightly tired or exhausted than to be fully awake and rested and have to carry around the truth. In the deepest exhaustion she feels him nearby, can fool herself for a minute or two.

One bad morning a couple of weeks ago, after a particularly bad series of nights, she had stood at the window and stated aloud her desire to die. Almost as soon as the words were out of her mouth a hummingbird had flown right up to the window and looked at her. She knew it was her son, but she wasn’t sure of the meaning: was he coming for her? Was he telling her to be strong? Was he reminding her that he was always there with her? This is another always-problem: she sees the signs but doesn’t know what they mean.

She will tell her sister about the man-angel in the fur coat she saw on the bus this morning at the same time as the fatal accident. Her sister always indulges her, maybe believes her, but also looks at her with worry and pity.

“Try anti-depressants. I know they will help you,” she constantly told her.

“You are going to have to reinvent yourself,” her brother’s wife had said. “If that had happened to me, I would move to another city – maybe even another country – and start over.”

Where would I go? Even now with a missing son and a crackpot president who wants them all gone, how could she leave the place where her entire family has settled? As time wears on, however, with each day heavier than the one before, as she walks from one end of her tiny apartment to the other with less and less reason to do so, she understands better the wisdom behind escape and reinvention.

Where once she had felt joy and passion and warmth and kindness, now sadness and anger are her steady companions, following her everywhere, to her family’s houses, climbing onto the bus with her, and all the way to her work. She finds herself in the oddest places doing the strangest things: sticking her head in the freezer at a convenience store, lying on the sidewalk and slamming her forehead into it, or beating her fists into her body just to feel the pain. Even if any of these gestures offer a brief relief, they are never enough to drown the other pain. She screams and yells and shouts into windows and pillows and closets and still it sits heavy within her, hardening her everything.

This is why I don’t keep a gun in the house, she jokes. She has told no one about her daily fantasy of shooting her own head off with a shotgun, cartoon style. She is not planning her death; it simply is the only way she can shut out the film that keeps replaying in her head.

Recalculating.

She does not want to take medication, but she also can’t figure out how she is expected to survive this most awful thing.

Recalculating.

It is still Wednesday morning.

When the bus spits her out downtown, she walks to the YMCA so she can drop into the pool and let the overheated water melt away her private and public sadnesses. When she catches herself thinking about all that is wrong, she counts black tiles on the pool’s floor.

*

It is finally Wednesday afternoon. Demi buys a coffee from the small café around the corner from the clinic where she has managed to be working as a medical assistant for the last four weeks, where she has been for the last five hours.

This coffee is her reward for maintaining.

“How’s your day going so far?” asks Keli, who looks to be in her early twenties, is excruciatingly thin, and spells her name with a heart dotting the ‘i’.

“It’s all right, you know,” Demi responds in a lilt that has never been hers.

Keli’s head pops up. “You enjoying the warm weather?”

“It’s a welcome relief from the rain, ya?”

Keli smiles. “I hate the rain! Your accent is so pretty. Where are you from?”

Without ticket or itinerary, Demi climbs into her new window seat. “Ireland,” she replies.

“Ooh, I love Ireland.”

“You’ve been?”

“No, but I want to go one day.”

“Ya, definitely worth the journey.”

“Name for your order?” asks Keli.

“Nuala.”

“N-o-o-l-a?”

“N-u-a-l-a.”

“Oh Nala! Like in The Lion King!”

“Something like that.” Demi smiles. “Thank you, Keli with a heart,” she says and walks to the side counter to wait for her coffee.

Born in Lebanon, Laila Halaby is the author of a memoir, The Weight of Ghosts, two novels, Once in a Promised Land and West of the Jordan (PEN Beyond Margins), and two collections of poetry.

A Boy and His Dog

by Joanne C. Hillhouse

✦

She swells and goes stiff so quickly that when they try to bury her, just moving her is a nightmare. Her eyes are filmy like the eyes of dead fish. Maybe that’s what happens to the eyes of all dead things. He wouldn’t know. He’s never seen death up close before. Except for the mounds of dead fish he sees waiting to be scaled and gutted while he tries not to gag on the ryshe smell when he’s dragged to the market with his father. His father drags him everywhere. On runs in the rusty pick-up he uses to move junk for clients – furniture, construction waste, top soil. On runs for his mother – “pick up some fish for me on your way home, I want to make some braaf”. He doesn’t usually volunteer to go, it’s just a given; the trials of being the youngest…and the only boy.

He and his father are both soaked in sweat from digging and lifting her into the hole when Mr. Patty, one of his father’s more cantankerous tenants, his balcony overlooking the grassy courtyard, starts shouting at them.

“I hope you all not planning to bury that thing there nuh” and since they had been planning to bury that thing there, had spent the last half hour digging a hole deep enough for the job, they have no rebuttal. Funny thing, the man had watched them digging in the stinging Saturday sun without complaint, comment, or even apparent interest from right there on his balcony. But this is not the boy’s first encounter with bad mindedness, with people who love to cause others botheration, just because. He has sisters after all.

He and his father end up forcing her stiff body into one of the big garbage bags his father uses for the blue bins, the ones that service all the tenants in the yard, loading it into the used-to-be-red pick-up, and driving it to one of those places where other people had already dumped things – “illegal dumping” the environmental lady who’d come to their school that time had called it. They leave Marmalade there.

The yard seems bigger without her in it, even with the apartments that form a quarter moon around the patchy mound of grass and, at the edge of it, the sandpit with the leaning shack made of plywood and coconut bough she’d once called home. One end of her heavy chain is wrapped around the base of the coconut tree; the other lies on the ground. Off to the side is the hole she was to be buried in, the hole he and his father never get around to re-filling. He wonders if his father, like him, half-hopes the botheration neighbor-tenant falls in coming home some night after work.

He’s never known a world without Marmalade in it, remembers squeezing through the sliding kitchen door, barefoot and diapered, to feed her his leftovers, not old enough yet to say when he needed to go but old enough to know the dog needed food. Everybody in the yard fed Marmalade, his sisters, his mother, his father, the tenants (most of them) – sometimes as many as six of them at a time. Marmalade grew to be big and muscled, her coat thick, almost too thick for this island sun, healthier looking than those dogs on TV – the ones in ads where people who were shocked (!) that the meal in the big bag of dog food wasn’t real meat made his mother choops. Imagine fussing over something like that. The only store bought dog food Marmalade ever got was the leftover beef stock, lots of bone, very little meat, from the butcher at the supermarket – if there was enough money to get it, and his father remembered.

Marmalade ate as well as they ate, plenty when there was plenty, if the tenants paid on time, if people needed things moved, if something wasn’t broken down or in need of fixing, and there was always something broken down and in need of fixing; little bit when there was little bit, a steady diet of leftovers. The people on American TV would be shocked!

But Marmalade was happy; he knew that. Always happy to see him; dancing around in circles in a way that looked silly on a dog her size, too excitable to take for walks too often even as he himself grew into something a little more than a too small boy and a string bean. He is 12 now, and has the muscles to prove it. But just when it seemed like things might even out, that he’d soon be big enough to manage the dog everyone was afraid of, so they wouldn’t have to keep her chained so much, so that he could take her on walks more often and not only let her off the leash when he was able to coax his father into taking them on a beach-run, just them, not his sisters, and not on one of the more popular beaches but somewhere Marmalade could run herself tired, she started getting tired.

After a while for the first time in her long life she was taken to a vet, a nice woman who touched her gently with a sad little smile, seeming to diagnose her just from looking at her.

“She’s old,” the woman said.

As if that was that. She was old; there was no cure for old; old things died.

They gave her the medicine given, and though she clearly wanted to, she was too weak to fight them, but it didn’t help. They had known it wouldn’t but he’d hoped anyway. Didn’t matter.

Marmalade, with her thick orange fur freckled with black, her thunder-clap bark, her unconditional love, died, and the boy felt sad, though he didn’t understand what he was feeling or why, because, to quote his sisters, it was just a stupid dog, who worked up themselves so over a stupid dog.

His father takes him for a drink after they dump her, his first beer, and a “don’t tell your mother”; a moment between men, and though he’d always felt that his father, like the rest of his family, hadn’t cared for Marmalade one way or the other, he feels happy, until feeling happy makes him feel sad again. He hates the taste of beer but manages to bluff his way through three solid swigs. His father rubs his head with a chuckle, as close to a hug as they ever get, this isn’t American-TV-world after all, and finishes off the rest of the beer for him in one long swallow.

Then one day, out of the blue, his father brings him a replacement puppy from God knows where. His mother, probably relieved to be rid of Marmalade, puts up a little resistance. But it isn’t one of his parents’ badder fights, it doesn’t feel like an earthquake, or have his sisters whispering about “divorce”, so he ignores everything and scratches at the pup’s belly, lifts it, nuzzles it against his cheek.

It’s a fluffy orange little thing that might easily have been a nephew, something close but not offspring close, to Marmalade. If Marmalade had had any pups; she hadn’t.

And as his mother fusses half-heartedly and his father enthusiastically tries to coax a smile out of her, pulling her into a hug while she squirms and pushes him away, his heart swells up with love for his parents, swells with love for everything, even his sisters, who seem equally enamored with the pup, because all babies are cute. He realizes they haven’t hit or teased him once since Marmalade’s death, all of them treating him in tender ways because they might not have cared about the stupid dog that they complained did nothing but shit up the place, but they knew he, the baby of their family, loved her inside out.

He’s sniping zombies while he walks the new pup. Rex, that’s what they call him, is no more tame than Marmalade had been but he’s big enough and the pup small enough that it doesn’t matter. He wishes he knew how to train him to heel and sit like the dogs on American-TV but in his world, his attempts to make the new puppy a house dog died after he came home to find their living room couch out in the front yard “renk with piss” as his mother ranted, the pup tied with a piece of rope to Marmalade’s tree, head cocked, eyes darting about, tail brushing the sand, as if wondering what all the fuss was about. After he’d cleaned up the couch and given up the mission to domesticate Rex, he decided he’d at least take him for walks every day. On one of the runs his father still drags him on, in what he’s starting to realize with some horror is an effort to teach him the family business, he talked his father into making a detour to the pet store for a puppy sized chain and collar. Now he makes a daily exercise of the task of walking the dog. But walking is boring, so he kills zombies while he walks. He’s won the game so many times, though, it’s not much of a distraction; at this point he could kill zombies with one eye closed and with only one steady hand which is good because the pup is a tugger, darting about at every distraction.

When he looks up to see what’s snagged Rex’s attention this time it’s a butterfly, a monarch, he thinks; he’d studied them in school. Monarchs are the regal looking ones with the golden wings, black stripes, and polka dot fringe, like a queen, always dressed for a dance. The monarch is perched on a leaf, the way butterflies always perch as though poised for flight at the first brisk breeze or sign of danger. But yet it sits there as Rex approaches, black nose within sniffing distance. And he finds himself staring at them, the new puppy and the butterfly that will be dead in a matter of weeks, if something doesn’t get it first. It clearly has no survival instincts. Rex is almost kissing, or preparing to eat, it now; he’s that close. And still the butterfly sits there.

The boy tugs hard on the leash, jerking the pup’s whole body, startling the monarch into flight. Except the orange-gold monarch darts around his face, its wings a caress, before properly taking off; and the boy stands there, the video game controller hanging limply from one hand, the leash clenched tight in the other, and though he isn’t aware of it, tears are streaming down his face.

Something Happened to My Heart that I Cannot Explain to You

by Hassana Shuaibu

✦

When I bump into you, my steps are hurried and my veil is brought low to cover my face. I do not want the baby clothes seller to see me. Your little frame tumbles on the muddy ground and I kneel to lift you up. I look back to see if the baby clothes seller noticed me, but she is busy with a woman on a stool, tiny shoes perched on her protruding stomach, her smile sunny from what I guess is light from within.

I turn to you and stare into a face I know but can’t place. I look around to search for your mother or sibling, anyone heading to you, but no one is coming our way.

“Are you looking for your Mummy?” You nod.

“Who has this child?” My voice rises above customers and buyers haggling prices. Few people turn to look at me. No one answers.

“What is your name?” You mouth something but the noise of the market carries the sound away.

I look at the setting sun. Soon, people will leave and silence will settle in.

“Where is your Mummy?” You point at me and giggle. I wonder if you understand me at all.

“Come, let’s go and find her,” I beckon and you place your hand in mine.

I look down at our hands for a heartbeat and shrug your hand away. Somehow it feels wrong to hold a child’s hands.

“When you see your mother, just let me know, okay?” You nod.

We trudge on, you ahead, so I can watch you. I speak to vendors and customers and they wave me away like a thief stealing time from customers who will pay. I point at women and tap their shoulders, and you shake your head each time. Until you point at a woman in deep conversation. When I tap her, she turns to me with a smile and I groan. It is the baby clothes seller.

******

“Is she your Mummy?” I ask you. You shake your head and I squeeze my eyes shut.

“Then why did you point at her?” My voice rises but you giggle like I have said something funny.

I tell her I am looking for your mother and she smiles at me like I am a cute beggar asking for food.

“How are you?” she asks.

“I am okay,” I stretch a smile across my face.

“Madam,” she whispers. “God will bring another one. You will be better by God’s grace.”

My breathing rises and I grab your hand. I push people in my way, blind to where I am going.

As we walk, I think about myself on a stool, and the shoes on my protruding stomach and the sunny smile on my face that came from light within. The stain that alarmed me when Ojoma left, still fist-sized in my stomach. The stares I caught, as the neighbors and friends and sisters and mothers and the baby clothes seller looked at each other when I wiped my tears. The smiles that followed when I whispered Ojoma’s name, like I was a cute beggar asking for food. The stories, they all told me, of mothers who lost their children when they were older and the pain that I was lucky to miss – the loss of a child already grown. The scolding that followed of my ungratefulness and impatience and failure of a test decreed. Until silence enveloped me, then seeped in to strangle Ojoma’s name. Until something happened to my heart that I cannot explain to you.

*******

You stumble on a stone and I rush to pick you up. You spring back up with a giggle and hug my legs. You look up at me, your eyes wide, and I am struck by the familiarity of your face. Anger rises at your mother.

“Just where is she?” I mutter. “Why have I not come across her, her scarf loosened, her face wet as she looks for you, frantic and screaming? How is the market not loud with worry and grief that a child has been lost?”

You point at someone behind me and I turn in a hurry, but it’s only the man selling lollipops on a tray.

“Is he your mother?” I laugh. You shake your head and laugh too.

I buy two lollipops and give you one. You place your hand in mine and we walk together. We dodge hawkers and boys pushing wheelbarrows and it feels like there is nothing better than the feeling of a child’s hand in mine.

********

The police station at the edge of the market is open but no one is in. We wait on the stairs, and you sit beside me, the lollipop in your mouth. When you raise your hands up, I place you on my lap, and the lollipop falls from your hand to the sand.

You wail and I look around, frantic. The market is busier – customers and vendors are trying to make last-minute sales.

“It’s okay, stop crying,” I say.

“See, I have another one,” I smile and hold the second lollipop.

“No,” you say and push it away. For a moment, I am stunned to hear you speak. Then you cry and cry and place your head on my shoulder and rock back and forth, until you quiet down to sniffles and wipe your tears and smile at me.

“You’re a brave girl, aren’t you?” I smile and you giggle. Your eyes light up and I envy your bravery – how you cried when I told you to stop.

“What is your name?” I pinch your cheeks.

“Ojoma,” you say and smile a smile I know.

*****

The policeman meets me at the stairs and sees me alone. You left with the last traces of the sunlight.

“Why are you crying here?” he asks, hands on his waist.

“I still miss her,” I say. “I still miss her.”

I cry and cry, and rock back and forth, until I quiet down to sniffles and wipe my tears and something happens to my heart that I cannot explain to you.

Two, Twenty-One, Thirty-Three

by Diane Lee

✦

Two

When my daughter was two, I took her to the pool for the afternoon. It was something we did often in the heat of Adelaide’s summer. I’d pack the esky with curried egg sandwiches wrapped up tight in tin foil to keep them fresh, raspberry cordial frozen solid in a large plastic bottle that melted as the day progressed and chunks of watermelon in an orange Tupperware container, found in a thrift shop for a song, all supplemented with icy poles from the pool’s canteen. I’d find the shadiest gum tree, stake out my turf and unfold the beach chairs on a tartan blanket near the toddler pool. My book would make an appearance, as would friends, sometimes.

She’d splash around in the shallowest end of the toddlers’ pool in her coloured one-piece swimsuit, hair hanging in damp ringlets around her plump face, playing with the other children, making lifelong friends that only lasted for a few hours. Late in the afternoon, when the orange sun was sinking gracefully into the city skyline, and the shadows of the gums lengthened across the pool, I called her. She’d been playing with another toddler, a delicate girl with dark hair and pale skin and a man who, with the same dark hair and pale skin, was clearly her father. I had looked up from my book periodically, happy that my daughter was happy.

‘Come on,’ I said this day. ‘It’s time to go home.’

She looked directly at me and said: ‘No. I want to go with them.’

I laughed.

Of course she did.

She was two.

Twenty-One

The silk of my dress rustled as I walked out into the cool of that April night, my cheeks flushed from the champagne and warm reception of the speech I had just given.

Inside, the room had been a blur of balloons and laughter, underpinned by oohs and ahs as photographs of the birthday girl at various ages were projected onto a wall.

There she was, asleep in her bassinet, the lemon-yellow blanket I crocheted tucked tight around her. There she was under the Christmas tree, grinning as she unwrapped presents, torn paper crumpled and strewn around her. There she was on the first day of school, in her checked brown uniform, blonde curls peeking out from under her straw hat. There she was in the black polo shirt with the red chicken logo, the uniform of her first job. There she was before her school formal in her gold dress, boyfriend’s arm around her slim waist. There she was at thirteen, sixteen, then twenty. Twenty-one years of her life, our lives, flickering past every few seconds.

Family and friends came to me throughout the evening, gushing about my girl.

You must be so proud.

I was.

She’d made it. She was twenty-one. Officially an adult. And I did it on my own.

Months earlier, I had tried to bribe her out of having a party. As I did every birthday. I offered her a passport and flights to Hong Kong. It would have been easier. Cheaper, too. But she wanted a party – You only turn twenty-one once, Mum, she’d said – so I capitulated. What’s the worst that could happen?

But looking at her tonight, glorious in the dress she’d bought especially for the party, the one we’d bought together, surrounded by everyone who loved her, I was glad she had won.

By the time I settled the bar tab, the room was almost empty. Presents had been gathered and carried to her boyfriend’s car. Streamers and glitter littered the floor. Half-eaten canapés had gone cold on silver trays. Champagne glasses, lipstick pressed into their rims, stood abandoned on the bar. Tealights extinguished.

Like the images projected on the wall, the party was already becoming something that had once happened, annexed to all the other memories of my daughter.

I stepped outside just as the brakes of the hired mini-bus hissed against the kerb and the doors swung open. I didn’t know a bus was hired to take people to a new location. And I never found out who hired it.

Her friends and boyfriend’s family surged towards it, laughing and giggling and calling to each other, ready to carry the celebration into the city. I could hear them tell the grinning driver which club they would go to first, arguing amongst themselves.

Breakers! No, Indigo. No, that doesn’t get going until one. How about The Tavern?

My daughter’s school friends, still finding their seats on the bus, were beckoning me. So were her boyfriend’s relatives.

Come on, they said. It’ll be fun!

I lifted the hem of my dress, smiling, and took a step toward the open door.

A hand caught my arm, and it wasn’t a gentle grip. It was my daughter, her fingers digging deep into my forearm.

I turned to her, expecting the happy, luminous face I’d been watching all evening. For a moment I couldn’t reconcile what I saw with the photographs still playing inside my head. Her features had twisted into a sneer.

You’re not fucking going.

She stared at me, blue eyes narrow, almost black with anger.

You’re not fucking coming with us.

I glanced towards the bus. People were still climbing aboard. Someone laughed. Someone called her name. No one was looking at me now.

I don’t remember what I said. Perhaps I said nothing. What was there to say?

I remember stepping back from the bus, the night air not explaining the chill that had lodged itself in my stomach.

I remember seeing her screaming at her boyfriend who was trying to placate her: She’s not fucking going.

I remember seeing her boyfriend comforting her.

I remember thinking, who is this person screaming at me? What have I done?

I remember thinking, I don’t understand.

I didn’t get on that bus.

We didn’t speak for three weeks after that night. Eventually the chasm of silence was bridged by a reluctant truce and life went on. There were other birthdays, other celebrations, other opportunities to believe whatever happened that night could be papered over, somehow repaired. That whatever had happened that night could be forgiven, forgotten. After all, she was still the daughter I knew and loved and raised.

I didn’t know that one night in April was an ending.

I didn’t know it would be years before I understood why.

I didn’t know that when I think of my daughter now, I see only the open doors of a bus I was never meant to board.

That I was not fucking going.

Thirty-Three

The door on the left opened, and as the Judge entered the courtroom, the Clerk said: All rise. The Mother stood, breathing deep, stilling jangled nerves. She bowed to the Judge.

The Daughter was there, on the left, sitting with her Husband and her Solicitor. The Barrister was already at the bar table, laptop open.

Their eyes met. The Daughter was the first to look away.

The Clerk beckoned the Mother to the bar table and she sat on the right, on her own. She pulled a lime green folder labelled “Court” out of her bag and opened it to the first tab.

Between the Barrister and the Mother was a lectern, microphone on. No water. The air was chilly, and the Mother pulled her jacket closer around her body.

The Barrister stood and pitched her argument.

There was no existing relationship between the Mother and the Grandchildren, she said. The Grandchildren did not know her. Any relationship introduced now would be unfamiliar to them and potentially disruptive. It would be damaging, especially to the Daughter.

The Mother listened.

There was no relationship.

She almost laughed.

There was no relationship because she was not allowed one.

The Judge peered at the papers in front of him and shuffled them.

The Mother waited for him to speak, to counter what the Barrister was saying, to recognise the ludicrousness of the situation.

She had turned this moment over in her head, imagining different scenarios. She had imagined being asked about her Grandchildren and the six years she had missed. She imagined the Judge acknowledging that those years were impossible to claw back. That the children, all grown up, would blame the Mother for decisions that were not hers to make.

Instead, the Judge spoke about the present.

The court could only deal with what was before it now.

He blinked.

The Mother wondered how many years could disappear in the time it took a person to blink.

Six years.

Ten.

Twenty.

Her Grandchildren were growing while the Mother was barred from their lives.

She thought of birthdays.

School concerts.

Christmas mornings.

Lost teeth.

Those first days of life, sweet-smelling and wrapped tight.

The Mother looked at the Judge, then at the folder containing the history of everything that had happened between her and the Daughter.

She thought about the word now.

Now, there was no relationship.

Now, her Grandchildren did not know her.

Now, the court would decide whether they should.

But children do not live in now. They live in birthdays and school terms and summers and Christmases. They live in the slow accumulation of ordinary days.

Now, there was a new court date.

Now, the hearing was over.

Now, the Mother gathered up her folder.

Behind her, the Daughter huddled with her Husband, the Barrister and the Solicitor.

The Mother stepped into the corridor.

She did not know what would happen next.

But she did know this: the years would keep coming.

And her Grandchildren would count them.

Some day.

Open Hearted

by S. Nadja Zajdman

✦

It was bitter, the winter of 1959. An epidemic of Hong Kong flu raged through the city. The disease felled Mum, Daddy and me. We were compelled to quarantine. Who would take care of my infant brother Michael? My mother’s sister and her husband weren’t an option. My father’s siblings couldn’t be trusted with anything. Through a network of neighbours, a woman was located on Goyer Street. Her name was Katarina Trautmann. In an audacious dash for freedom, Herr and Frau Trautmann, with their two teenage daughters, escaped East Berlin in 1953.

Katie Trautmann was born in Aachen in 1912, one of four daughters. There may have been a little brother who died. In 1916 her father was killed on a battlefield in France. Their widowed mother raised Katie and her sisters. In the 1930s Katie was working as a maid in a wealthy home in Berlin. Friedrich Trautmann was a guest. That is how they met. In Berlin, Friedrich Trautmann was an engineer and during the war was recruited as an officer into the Wehrmacht. According to what he told Mum, during the war he held a position and was posted to a place where he would not have participated in atrocities, but he was aware that atrocities were being committed. After the war he was considered skilful enough to be of value to the Russians, who conscripted him and flew him to the Soviet Union where, for two years, he served a form of slave labour. In Montreal, Friedrich Trautmann earned a modest living as a mechanic.

The Trautmanns’ eldest daughter, Helga, was born in 1938. Doris was born on December 8, 1940. I always remember Doris’ birthday because it is two days after mine.

In the depths of winter, at the start of 1959, the Trautmanns sheltered and cared for my baby brother Michael. Katie Trautmann grew to love my brother like the son she never had. She rocked him and held him to her ample breasts and placed him in a carriage positioned in front of a sunlit window, as if he were a plant. When my parents recovered and were once more able to care for Michael, reluctantly, Mrs. Trautmann returned him.

Two years later an apartment across the hall from the Trautmanns’ apartment became available, and we moved to 2975 Goyer St. apartment fourteen Montreal Quebec! I had to memorize this address in case I got lost.

“If you can’t find your way home, then you go and find a policeman.” Mum instructed. “The policeman is your friend. He will bring you home.”

I recall our first evening in the new apartment. I was five years old. Mum was on her hands and knees, scrubbing the floor of the room that, for the next seven years, I shared with Michael. Mrs. Trautmann lugged a mop and a bucket. She was helping; scouring and cleaning the adjacent floors. As I watched the two women it seemed to me that I must do my part, so I fetched my toothbrush, filled a cup with water, sloshed the water onto the floor, plopped onto my hands and knees, and began scrubbing away with my toothbrush.

Mum stopped scrubbing in mid-scrub. Mrs. Trautmann’s mop stopped moving.

“Slodka.” Mum whispered. Then she translated. “Sweetheart.”

“Kinde.” Mrs. Trautmann agreed. Almost in unison, they appealed. “Don’t help.”

Mum and Mrs. Trautmann spoke German to each other and English to me. They never addressed each other by their first names. Forever formal, the two Europeans called each other “Mrs. Zajdman” and “Mrs. Trautmann.”

While Mum and Dad worked, Mrs. Trautmann greeted us at lunchtime with sometimes hot and always lovingly prepared meals. I recall coming home in winter to steaming bowls of chicken noodle soup. We had chicken noodle soup often because it was Michael’s favourite. I also recall sandwiches of linseed bread lined with thin slices of spicy German salami.

After school the door to our apartment, and to Mrs. Trautmann’s, was always open to us. Michael grew so attached to Mrs. Trautmann that he began telling people she was his grandmother. He felt guilty about doing so, but wasn’t sure why. Instinctively he knew not to repeat this to Mum. Instead, Michael confessed to me. I heard his confession, and then pronounced, “That’s not the way it works.”

“But I want a grandmother! Why can’t I have a grandmother?! All the other kids have one!”

“Because! A grandmother is a mother who is your mother’s mother or your father’s mother, except that Mummy doesn’t have a mother and Daddy doesn’t have a mother, so you can’t have a grandmother. Anyway, we’ve got a grandmother. She’s hanging on the wall.”

“You mean the lady in the picture? But she’s not real.”

“Well, she used to be in real. In ‘Before The War’ she used to be real.”

The term Holocaust was not yet in common usage, but I had heard of The Land of Before The War. It was a mysterious place my parents returned to when they spoke in Polish.

Michael refused to accept a photo facsimile. “But I want a real grandmother now!”

“Look.” My little brother was cute, but he could be exasperating.
“We have our parents but they don’t have theirs. So who should cry; them or you?”

“Oh! I never thought of it that way.”

Over Jewish holidays, unless Daddy was called upon to lead a Seder, we were never invited to extended family, but we always shared Christmas with the Trautmanns. Their Christmas tree was our Christmas tree. On Christmas Eve, Michael was accorded the honour of sticking the star onto the top branch of the tannenbaum.

“Allez h-up!” Mr. Trautmann would hoist Michael and lift him level to the top of the tree. Nervously Michael fumbled among the decorations, until he managed to insert the star-shaped piece of foil onto a top branch.

“Bravo!” Mr. Trautmann declared, while Mrs. Trautmann applauded. “You did this gut!” Michael would thrust out his little chest and beam with pride. Then he’d be lowered back down onto the ground.

I would profit by this Hallmark moment by sneaking a piece of chocolate from an open box on the coffee table. Invariably Mrs. Trautmann caught me at it. “Sharon! You will get fat! You want to be an actress! You must have disziplin! You cannot be fat!”

“Ach Katie!” Generally silent, Mr. Trautmann sprang to my defence. “It’s Christmas! Leave the child alone!” Like my daddy, Mr. Trautmann was on my side.

My recollections of Helga are vague. Early she married a German businessman named Herr Lothar and returned with him to Germany. Helga looked like a sun-kissed Rhine maiden, but Doris was dark, and looked like Romy Schneider. She had a brief first marriage to a man named Herr Apfel. Michael and I called him Mister Apples. Mister Apples worked in a chocolate factory. I thought Doris was wondrously lucky to be married to a man who worked in a chocolate factory! He brought us samples, and Mrs. Trautmann stressed their quality. These were no adulterated Oh Henry bars.

“Kinder, das is DEUTSCHE schokolade!” I was sad when Doris left Mister Apples. There would be no more schokolade.

I was born slightly lame. From Germany Mrs. Trautmann imported, or perhaps it was Helga who sent over Franz Joseph Wasser. Mrs. Trautmann believed this spring water had healing properties. Nightly, either Mrs. Trautmann or Mum would massage my feet with the emperor’s elixir, and then they had me walk up and down the living room floor with pencils between my toes. Each evening I had to walk up and down the living room floor with pencils between my toes until, for me, from Germany, Mrs. Trautmann imported the first of Dr. Scholl’s arch-supported wooden sandals. In the hours after school, after I finished my homework and before my parents came home, Mrs. Trautmann taught me to knit, and later, to crochet.

In summer, we were invited to the Laurentian Hills, to a place the Trautmanns called their farm. We were welcome to stay all summer. There were no animals on this farm. It was a primitive property. There was no indoor plumbing. Walking distance, there was an outhouse. A column of wax paper dipped in honey dangled from a hook on the ceiling over the table used for dining. Flies died there. Still, like kamikaze pilots, more kept flying through the open door off the porch, heading directly for the honeycombed column. I was struck by how stupid flies must be.

The bedrooms were located on an upper floor. The room I came to think of as mine had a slanted roof. It leaned in as if wanting to speak to me. On soft summer nights, tucked under a light quilt listening to jiminy crickets singing in the tall grass outside an open window, I felt cocooned in a fairytale.

On the shore of a nearby lake, wearing nothing but a sunhat and an undershirt, Michael would squat, scoop sand into a pail, and then he’d toss it out again. With floating devices strapped to our waists and Michael clinging to my hand, we’d be guided into the shallows of the lake where we’d splash and cool off. In nearby woods, in August, we’d pick berries that Mrs. Trautmann baked into late-summer pies. I noted that my little brother’s eyes were the same colour and shade as the berries, so I came to call him Blueberry Eyes.

With Michael and me ensconced on this oasis, my parents were free to take a break, even if they couldn’t afford a full-blown vacation. They scheduled a three-day getaway, but couldn’t see it through. After forty-eight hours alone together my mother wailed, “Oh Abram! I miss the kids!”

“O.K.” Daddy conceded. “Let’s go.” They got into the family Chevrolet, whose doors against the back seats were sealed so that Michael and I could never fall out, and took off for the Trautmanns’ farm.

“Oh. Hi Mummy.” Michael and I were perched on a hill within yelling distance of Mrs. Trautmann’s country kitchen, companionably sculpting mud pies. “What are you doing here?”

“Oh!” Mum threw herself on me. Ruefully, Daddy shook his head. “Oooo Mummy!” I protested and wriggled out of her embrace. “You’re going to make yourself all muddy!”

“Kinder!” From down the hill, Mrs. Trautmann trilled. “Kom! We have lunch!” Michael and I dropped our inedible pies and got up, mud dripping from our fingertips and, in Michael’s case, smeared across his cheeks and chin.

“You better wash your face before Mrs. Trautmann sees you.” I warned him.

“I have to go inside to wash my face and Mrs. Trautmann will see me but it doesn’t matter!” Michael responded logically, secure in Mrs. Trautmann’s unconditional love. We headed down the hill, trailing mud, while our parents trailed behind us. Mum was sniffling. Daddy had his arm around her. “What’s the matter with you, Mummy?”

“Nothing!” Mum blasted. “I’m just so happy to see you, that’s all!”

The offspring of Holocaust survivors tend to fault their parents for supposedly viewing them as replacements; surrogates for children cherished and lost. As an officer in the Wehrmacht, Mr. Trautmann didn’t see his younger daughter Doris for the first four and a half years of her life. When he returned from the front, he was a stranger to her. As a child, I sensed that Mr. Trautmann identified me with Doris. During my formative years, Mr. Trautmann seemed to see me as a second chance. For a former Wehrmacht officer, it was fitting that redemption came in the form of a daughter of Holocaust survivors.

Bewildered though he was, my little brother perceived something significant. This middle-aged German couple served as surrogates for the grandparents their war stole from us. Unstintingly they shared their homes, their holidays, and their hearts. Together Mrs. Trautmann and I watched and loved Lucy. While massaging my feet with Franz Joseph Wasser, Mrs. Trautmann would regale me with the tale of Doris Day, whose original name was Doris Kappelhoff and who, as a teenager, with therapy and exercise, overcame a serious leg injury and went on to sing and dance in the movies. Excitedly, she also told me about a hot new German film star who was taking Hollywood by storm. Because this actor had a name that could be played with, I called him Max The Million! (Though he was born in Vienna and raised in Zurich, for Katie Trautmann, Maximilian Schell was German.)

While Katie Trautmann prepared my meals, monitored my intake of chocolates, and focused on strengthening my feet, my Jewish survivor aunts played cards, went to their hairdressers, and bad-mouthed my mother for placing her children in the care of a woman they referred to as “a Nazi bitch.”

Decades later, shortly after both our birthdays, in front of a downtown Christmas display window I ran into Doris. She had married again. Her second husband was a Jewish divorcee with five children. Mrs. Trautmann was now step-grandmother to five Jewish children.

In front of a Christmas display window Doris stunned me by saying, “It must’ve been hard on your mother, having my mother take care of you.” Sincerely, with all my heart, I was able to tell her, “No. It wasn’t. Mum was grateful that we didn’t have to become latch key kids. We couldn’t depend on extended family. Because of your mother, we had someone to whom we could come home.”

Tongues Tangled in Transit

by Hannan Khan

The TV broadcasted America and the walls
broadcasted Bismillah.
Bills slipped under the door in snappy
foreign fonts, your ma pinned verses above the sink
with a canted nail. Ceiling fan spun like a
drained diplomat, mediating heat & memory.

Your pa chewed silence with his news, you

learned two postures: prayer & pornography,
forehead on mat, thumb on glass. At night,
the hallway rehearsed two berceuses — one from a

city. Sometimes, you kissed a lothario in the dark chat
window, then rinsed your mouth with holy ayahs,

beside bhindi wrapped in newspaper from another empire.

California, the phone spoke Punjabi, your contraband body
translated both into a small animal quaking.
You drilled to switch your heartbeats like
cursed channels, fast for lust, slow for God, mute for family.
Now the house is hushed. The TV hums like an ocean in exile,
the verses peel like senescent scabs. You lie
on the floor, listening for a third lingo — not īmān,
not state — just a tongue that hisses: stay.

Cropped

by R.K. Patel

Noor came into the kitchen while I was cutting oranges and asked if I still had the custody file. She said it too formally, one shoulder against the fridge, one sock half off her heel, as if the kitchen had rules she had studied. Her phone lay faceup on the table. It had already lit twice with her father's name, silent.

Before I could answer, she asked me to listen once without interrupting.

I told her I could listen. This was not the same as promising not to help, but I heard it as a courtesy, proof that language could still open a room to me.

On the counter, four wedges waited where I had left them, juice drying on the board. The dishwasher kept clicking to itself.

Noor sat at the table and put both hands in her lap. "I know what happened before," she said.

I waited. She looked at the salt shaker. "I know about the Lantern. I know Dad drove after he drank. I know about the pantry door. I know about my wrist."

The order was poor, but not unusable. She began with incidents rather than fear, which made them sound like exhibits brought in from another room. Still, I understood the choice. Dates steadied people. Objects steadied people. A door, a car, a wrist. They gave the listener somewhere to place disbelief.

For twelve years I had interpreted for people whose lives depended on being believed in the right order. In a clinic in Elizabeth, a woman from Lahore kept placing the first threat after the funeral, meaning a neighbor's funeral, not her brother's. The lawyer asked for the month. The woman gave the smell of the bus station, her son's uniform, lentils soaking in a pressure cooker. None of this was wrong. It was only memory arranging itself before it knew it needed to be useful.

I translated faithfully at first. Then I learned to do it well.

Noor cleared her throat. "I'm not saying you made it up."

"I know."

"I know you were right to—" She closed her eyes. "I know I'm not supposed to forget it just because he's better now."

"Better," I almost said. The word came up behind my teeth and stayed there. She had heard the weakness and removed it before I could. By the third pass, the woman from Lahore had learned to say March first, then bus station, then lentils, her mouth flattening around each item because order was another fee she had agreed to pay.

Her phone lit again. I did not look. Tariq would not write anything open to objection. He would say "take your time," or "no rush," or "I'm downstairs," as if patience were a condition he had earned in the loading zone.

"Are you asking me to go with you tomorrow?" I said.

Noor looked confused, and for one second I mistook it for relief. The meeting was with Ms. Klein, the evaluator assigned after Noor told the court she wanted to change the arrangement before graduation. She would be eighteen in forty-one days, a number I had begun carrying like a prescription I did not intend to fill.

"No," she said. "I need you not to."

I cut one orange wedge in half, though there was no reason to make it smaller. The knife had a line of pulp along its edge. "Then what are we doing?"

She rubbed her thumb across her wrist. The mark had been gone for years, but the gesture had remained; or perhaps I only saw it now because I needed evidence of her body agreeing with mine. "I need the picture."

I did not ask which picture. That would have been another kind of rehearsal.

Later, in my own case, I had known what to place first. I did not invent Tariq. He had been drunk; he had punched the pantry door hard enough to split the frame; he had held Noor's wrist until the mark came up during television, while the remote balanced on her knee. He also remembered the name of every dentist she had disliked, salted rice after cooking because she said it tasted brighter, drove across town for the cheap blue quilt she had left at his apartment and then slept on the floor outside her room because she would not unlock the door. On paper, the wrist stayed. The quilt did not.

The folder was in the hall closet, inside the plastic box where I kept passports, expired warranties, batteries, the manual for a blender that had died on frozen mango, three keys to apartments we no longer had. Noor watched me stand. She did not thank me. She pulled the loose sock back over her heel.

When I came back, I set the folder on the table and kept my hand on it. The photograph was near the back. Cropped, it showed only her wrist and the small brown mark above the bone, no face, no room, no child, which had made it useful.

"I want to live with him until graduation," she said. "I know what happened. I know what could happen again." She looked at the phone, then at me. "I know what happens here."

There were corrections available. "Could" was better than "might," though it made danger sound theoretical. "Here" was too vague, and for one second I was grateful for the vagueness. "Dad" might have helped her, if she had still been using "Dad," but "him" was colder and probably wiser. I could have told her that the sentence was strongest if she stopped before explaining.

Instead I slid the photograph from the folder and placed it facedown beside her glass.

For a moment neither of us moved. Tariq's name appeared and disappeared without sound. Noor turned the phone facedown.

She took the photograph.

The orange wedges were still on the board. I picked one up and put it in my mouth with the rind on.

Stranger to Stay

by Daniel Gordon

“Do you remember the noise of it? Grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins and great-grandparents. Daughters. Four generations at one point… It was too quiet with just me and mum here… But this is where I grew up. This is where I raised Camila.”

Emilia puts her hand to the wall of her home. The old paint job crumbles under her touch. She closes her eyes, and for a moment she hears the echoes of their voices, as if the walls still remember their vibrations. “So many years. All these memories… How do I say goodbye to all that?”

Her neighbour looks at his rubber slippers and tugs at them with his toes. “You don’t,” he says. “You just leave…”

It has been five months since Emilia’s mother died. Five months with only memories for company. She was 94 years old. Her neighbour has agreed to try to sell the house for whatever price he can get, though they both know nobody will want it. Empty houses are the one thing this city has in abundance.

“You’re doing the right thing, Emilia. She wouldn’t want you to be stuck here on your own. There’s nothing left. Go. God bless you. Be with your family.”

#

There are countless things Emilia couldn’t bear to part with, but had to anyway. Her mother’s diaries, her grandfather’s violin, and Camila’s drawings from primary school: these and a thousand other impossible choices have left her with the heavy, lame-wheeled suitcase she is now dragging along the dusty road.

Emilia takes a side street, where she’s unlikely to run into anyone she knows, though there is hardly anybody left to run into. It is early in the morning, and already the sun is beating down with uncommon heat. She walks close enough to the row of buildings to stay in the shade, but far enough to avoid materials that might come crashing from collapsing rooftops. Above her, a tree sprouts from the rotten wood of a battered balcony, half its roots dangling into the air. It is bound to fall one of these days.

An old dog raises itself as she walks by. It looks at her as if to beg for food, but can quickly tell she has none to give and lies back down. Emilia passes the old ice cream parlour, where she used to take Camila for a treat when she came home with good grades. Its windows are brown with thick grime, and she can barely see through them even when cupping her hands against the glass. There is a broken table. A few stray chairs.

By the time Emilia arrives at the city’s main square, her back is damp with sweat and her suitcase dirty from all the dust it has kicked up. There used to be music here, a few decades ago. Always one group or another, trying to get friends or strangers to sing along to the rhythms they all grew up with. Artists would set up easels and chairs to paint the church or the statues, or the hill that overlooks the city. Kids would play catch around the fountains that still ran with water back then.

Emilia walks past a few drunk men, half-asleep on the stone benches, moving only to turn their backs to the sun. Next to the church, where the buses used to stop, she finds two bike taxis. They are welded together with parts of spare metal and old furniture for seats. The younger driver seems to be about 50. His gut swells past his unbuttoned shirt, and a permanent smile is tacked onto his face. The other man, much older, sits slumped with his back against the wall.

The old man is confused when Emilia stops in front of him. Shifting his weight around, he leans first on the ground, then on his knees for support to stand up. He straightens his crooked body as much as he can. The few teeth he has left jut from his gums at odd angles. People this age don’t work unless they really need the money.

“Can you take me to the station?”

“The station?” he asks, not quite believing someone would choose him over the younger man. The short-sleeved shirt he wears is full of holes. His bald head is crowned by angry, reddish-brown skin, peeling in parts. He sees her looking and takes a cap from his pocket to cover it. “Of course!”

The old man moves to prepare his bike, but the younger driver is quick to grab Emilia’s suitcase and tug at it, though she doesn’t let go. “You’ll be there in 30 minutes at most if I drive you. With him, it’ll take you all day.”

Hearing this, the old man’s face bunches into a fleshy bulb, yet he doesn’t say anything.

“I have all day,” Emilia insists, and wrestles the suitcase back.

The younger man’s grin widens, as if he’s just been told some funny joke. Spitting on the ground between Emilia and the old man, he turns his back to them.

The old man sticks his thumb into a hole at the hem of his shirt. He takes a trembling breath before giving Emilia a grateful nod. Stepping towards her, he tries to heave the suitcase onto the seat of the bike taxi, but finds that it is too heavy.

“Let me help you,” Emilia says, and together, they lift it up. The old man wheezes from the effort. “You’re sure it’s not too far to the station?” she whispers to him.

Chewing on the edge of his flaking lips, he frowns like he’s thinking about a hard riddle, then looks at his tattered shoes, stretching his toes within them. “Do you need to get there soon?”

“Not at all. I was so nervous today that I left six hours early.”

His expression changes into the kind of smile only the very young or the very old can muster. “We’ll get there,” he says.

The old man’s whole body curls as he begins to pedal. For a second, nothing happens. But then his leg stretches, and the bike begins to move. Emilia has no time to feel relieved before they stop at the second intersection next to the old theatre, where colonial pillars sustain a weathered roof. “I once played Sancho Panza in a production of Don Quijote when I was young and still had the stomach for it,” he says, pointing at the decrepit building. “And the gravedigger in Hamlet. I didn’t get many good parts like that, but that’s what I did. I was an actor.”

Next to them stands a statue with a determined westward gaze: the island’s national poet. It has held up well, despite the years of neglect; the roof above it less so. Water has pooled in a cavity between broken tiles and splintered wood, and drips slowly onto the statue’s head, following the path of slimy film down its marbled face.

“That’s nice,” Emilia says, now wondering whether six hours will be enough time to get to the station.

“You’re heading to the capital?” the old man asks, and pedals on.

“And flying onwards from there. I’m going to live with my daughter and my grandson.”

The effort of cycling interrupts their conversation before it can really get started. In any case, Emilia knows it is not a strange thing to leave the country. Many say it’s stranger to stay.

She keeps a guarding hand on her suitcase. They are going far too slow for it to slip from the seat, but there is an anxiety inside of her that flares up whenever she isn’t touching it. Camila has told her she’ll have to hand it over when she flies; that it will slide past one set of rubber curtains, only to reappear through another, 22 hours later and half the world away.

The old man pedals in two stages: first, he pushes hard to speed up, his bony fingers tightening around the handles and the strain of it bending his body. Then, when the bike has gathered some momentum, he leans back, trying to recover from the sprint, though they always slow down before he can catch his breath. Emilia knows there is a slope coming up half a mile ahead, not very steep but long and steady. “Can we take a break here?” she calls.

“Are you sure?”

“You see this?” She points to a large, square building. “That’s where I went to primary school. My daughter Camila, too.” Emilia gets off the bike, passing a big tree sprouting from the cracked pavement. She leans against the fence, careful not to scratch herself on its rusted spikes.

The old man pinches his shirt near his heart and tugs at it to cool his body. “As did I,” he says, beaming. “I think… ah yes, that one, with all the broken windows? That was my classroom. Which one was yours?”

“I don’t remember. But Camila’s was next to the entrance over there. I picked her up every day. Right here.”

They stand in silence for a moment. Sunlight draws the shape of leaves on the dried-out lawn, swaying to the rhythm of the wind.

“When did she leave?”

“Almost 20 years ago,” Emilia says. “She’s an architect. In Europe.”

He smiles at Emilia, as if he, too, were proud of Camila. “You mentioned a grandson?”

“Nine years old already,” she says, and out of habit, takes her phone from her pocket to show him the pictures. “The last time Camila visited was before he was born. She sent me this phone, and we talk all the time, but she works very hard. And she’s raising him all on her own. It’s not easy to get time off for such a long trip. And I could never go visit them either, with my mother to take care of…”

Emilia worries that she has said more than is proper. People usually avoid the sad undercurrent of it all. A wave of embarrassment prickles her skin, though the old man gives her no cause to feel it.

“You must be excited to see them.”

“I am,” she says, and nods, leaving the fear that paralyses her unmentioned: of living in a place with a strange language, where people will look at her for sounding foreign; where the freezing cold will burn her skin and the winter months will leave her starved for light. Where her daughter and her grandson will spend all day at work and school, and she will be alone in a small flat, with nothing to do except wait. A place where, Camila says, your neighbours might as well be strangers, and where people don’t speak to each other at the bus stop.

“What about you, do you have children?” she asks to stop herself from thinking about all this, though she already knows the answer.

Shaking his head, he adjusts his cap. “It wasn’t meant to happen for me.” The air has come to a standstill. The old man slaps the back of his neck and checks his hand. Flicking away a crushed mosquito, he nods at the bike. “Shall we?”

Emilia pictures her daughter running through the entrance at the ringing of the bell, her braided hair among the heads of children. She is waiting for some signal in her body. Some sense to tell her she is ready to leave, but it doesn’t come.

She lets go of the fence and pats the rusty residue from her hands.

#

A rotten stench rises from the river below. It has always been full of rubbish, but is now brimming with it. Only a few stray streams of dark water glitter between piles of plastic bottles, disintegrating clothes, and old children’s toys. There are pale bones of dead birds buried in their fluff of feathers. Emilia pinches her nose until they’ve passed it.

The old man speeds up before the incline begins, but within seconds, all the bike’s momentum has evaporated. There are no low gears to switch to, and his thin legs labour as they push. Drenched in sweat, his shirt clings to the bulging shape his back has hunched into. Soon, he is rocking back and forth, leveraging every ounce of his weight to fight his way up the slope. There’s a crackle to his wheezing breath.

“Please stop here,” Emilia calls, and steps out with one leg onto the road to prevent them from rolling down again. “I didn’t bring any water. And I would really like a drink. There’s a place over there.”

The old man turns to her, squinting from exhaustion. “I know it,” he says.

Two blocks along a perpendicular street, they arrive at a house with open doors. Several bedsheets have been stitched together into an improvised canopy, giving shade to a few tables and chairs on a dry lawn. The hum of a generator sounds out from behind the building.

“Would you watch over my suitcase for a second?” Emilia asks and walks into the house without waiting for an answer. A minute later, she comes out with two cold lemonades.

“For you,” Emilia says, placing the glass bottles on one of the tables.

The old man pushes the bike onto the lawn. “I can’t accept that.”

Emilia picks the closest chair to her suitcase. “You can. Where I’m heading, the money I have in my purse won’t buy me anything at all. Please just take a seat and have a drink.”

The old man wipes his face with his collar, though there is not a dry spot left on his shirt. He looks at his trembling hands and rubs his swollen knuckles. “Thank you,” he says, and sits opposite Emilia. He makes himself drink slowly, even though he clearly wants to gulp the whole bottle in one go.

Emilia slides her hand over the table. The wood is rough and worn down by weather and age. On the labels of their glass bottles, the shape of the island is printed, peeling from condensation. Lágrimas Negras, a song they’ve heard a million times, starts playing from a radio inside the house. It’s a sad song, but even the sad songs make you want to dance in this country.

“My family comes from back there,” he says, pointing with a shaky finger to the hills in the west. “I remember living there when I was still very young.”

“Is that so?” Emilia can’t see far through the shimmering air.

“We had grapevines growing all around. We made wine, sometimes even sold it. Not very good wine. It was much too sweet, I’m told. But it was something. And it was ours… This was a long time ago.”

Emilia keeps her eyes trained westward. She tries to imagine a patch of that hillside overgrown with vines, but finds that she can’t.

With a rattling cough, the old man loosens something in his lungs and swallows it with a sip of lemonade. Then he begins to bob his knees and hum to the rhythm and the melody of the music.

“Did you ever think about leaving?” Emilia asks.

The old man shrugs. He takes his hat off to fan himself with the cap and, leaning back into his chair, gives a gum-filled, cheeky smile. “You know the four groups of people who stay? They are: the stupid, the old, the poor, and the ugly. I’m a proud member of at least three of those groups. And the fourth one is up for debate,” he says, laughing. “But of course I’ve thought about it,” he continues, before Emilia can answer. “Who hasn’t? Sometimes I regret that I didn’t do it. But then I think back, and I remember that no matter how bad things got here, there was never anywhere else I wanted to be. I had family. I had friends. Most of them are long gone, of course, but even now…” He sticks his thumb into the mouth of the bottle and flicks it out with a melodic pop. “Look, I’m 79 years old. Memories are the one thing I have left in life. And this place, for all its faults… It helps me remember. I see those hills, and I remember growing up there. I drive past my old school, and I remember my first kiss. I was eleven, and she was called Isabel. I remember sitting at this very table, maybe eight years ago, with a good friend who has since died. We used to act together. Why would I want to be in a place where I have no history? No memories?”

Emilia takes a sip of her lemonade. The sweetness of it coats her tongue. She stares at the old man without offering an answer to his question.

“Oh, I’m sorry, I wasn’t trying to… I’m sure it’s different for you. You have your daughter and your grandson. Besides, you’re still young.”

Emilia has to laugh at this. “66 isn’t young.”

“It is to a 79-year-old.” He smiles and then, after a breath, leans forward to look at her closely. “You’ll have a good life there. You’ll see.”

“Touch wood,” she says, knocking on the table. “Camila has been wanting me to come for years. But I…”

“You had to take care of your mother,” the old man says.

Emilia bites her lip and shakes her head slightly. “I’m not even sure that is why I stayed. The house we lived in, it used to be full. A little too full, maybe. But then my nieces and nephews left, and pretty soon my brothers and my sister went to live with them. And Camila too… I know that they all did it out of necessity, but… I was angry. I never admitted it, but I was angry with each and every one of them. Not because they were leaving me or my mother behind, but because…”

Emilia grapples for words she can’t quite find. A bird flutters by and lands on the decaying roof of a house across the street. Its windows are boarded up. There are no materials to fix it. Even if there were, nobody would have the money to pay for it. And even if they did, there are no workers left to do the job.

“I don’t know whether there was ever any real chance of things getting better here. But maybe the whole reason it turned out like this is because everybody left. Maybe the problem is that nobody is willing to stay…” Shards of glass glitter in the puffs of dried grass around them. Emilia pulls the label off her bottle and tears the wet paper into little pieces. “I don’t even know if I believe that. Without the money Camila sent, there’s no way we could have…”

Emilia falls silent. The old man’s stained and tattered shirt is only now beginning to dry. The joints of his hands are swollen, and the peeling sores on his scalp shimmer with sweat.

“How do you do it?” Emilia asks.

The old man lifts his bottle as if it were an object of admiration, holding it by the tips of his fingers. One last sip of lemonade swirls at its bottom. “To be or to be. There is no question,” he says, and drinks what is left.

#

They push the bike up the rest of the hill. The station lies a few hundred metres below. Rushing air cools their bodies on their way down, and Emilia keeps a tight grip on her rattling suitcase. A sign tells them the bus bound for the capital will leave as planned in three hours. There is a small crowd at the station, more people than she is used to seeing together. Most of them are too old to leave, and have come to wave their family or friends off. They see Emilia’s suitcase and are quick to help her lift it from the seat.

“The capital, huh?” someone asks.

“And onwards from there,” Emilia says.

One of them is tuning a guitar that is missing the second string. He asks the crowd for suggestions, but nobody reacts until the old man calls out the song that played on the radio, halfway up the hill. The instrument is beaten down by age and has to be strummed hard to be heard over the nervous chatter, in which the impending farewell is becoming steadily harder to ignore.

Emilia checks her handbag for her passport and her purse. There is nothing left to do but wait. She wants to pay the old man, but he is so caught up in the music that she doesn’t want to interrupt. From behind his flaking lips, he sings along, at first mumbling, but then in a cracked baritone voice for the last repetitions of the chorus.

The silence that follows the final chord is immediately swallowed by noise. Only Emilia and the old man notice that the song is over. He lets out a happy sigh and turns to Emilia, bowing to her like he used to on stage. She pays him three times more than he asked for. “Thank you. For everything,” she says, and closes his left hand around the money. With things as they are, it might be worth half as much in a month. “Take it. It’s okay,” she adds before he can protest.

He takes her hand and holds it tight. “You’ll have a good life there. I know you will.”

Someone pulls out a chair for Emilia to sit. She closes her eyes, letting the voices around her melt into a blur. There is birdsong and a breeze, and the workings of the rusted bike pedals. The guitarist retunes a string. And it is only after the old man has disappeared around the corner that Emilia realises she forgot to ask his name.

The Beauty Parlour

by Hira Amir

One February, when the Karachi sky turned a groggy blue, and the koel birds were at their loudest with the koo-ooos, I started accompanying my mother to the beauty parlour two streets away from home. My mother was a woman of routines. She firmly believed that routines were the only anchor holding you down in life. Without them, she said, your soul can float away and lose grounding.

A monthly visit to the beauty parlour was a part of my mother’s self-care routine. This was when she sat in a tattered swivelling leather chair, and a lively woman hovered around her, scooping a viscous, syrupy substance from a plastic bowl, and slathering it on my mother’s face. The substance would dry quickly and look like volcanic ejecta, beneath which her skin would soften, heal, and become plump like a tomato.

The beauty parlour aunty would carefully pluck stray eyebrow hair from my mother’s face with a scientist’s precision, and end the facial by massaging a serum into her skin pores, where she had to freeze all her facial muscles for it to do its magic.

When it was my turn to sit in the swiveling leather chair that appeared gauntly with its peeling edges, I was already overwhelmed by the stinging smell of foaming bleach and cheap hair dye. Hair spray cans, skin whitening lotions, navy-blue tins of Nivea cream, and face serums lined the counter like soldiers. The brightness of the vanity lights magnified every pore and filament on my face and made me shrink in my chair. The beauty parlour aunty stood over me and knit her brows together as she inspected what needed to be done. I felt like a specimen caught between two glass films, being studied under a microscope.

“Look at the state of her upper lip. I’ve asked her to bleach it several times.” My mother pointed; her own face slick and plastered with a glossy sheen.

“No bleaching!” I screeched at the thought of it burning and stinging my skin.

“Threading will be just fine.” The woman decided.

I saw her pick up a spool of thread and expertly wrap some of it around her fingers, holding one strand between her teeth. Sneering like a lion that’s just found its prey, her face came close to me, too close for it to be comfortable, her cleavage an inch from my nose, in which nestled a small gold pendant in the shape of an F. Her name was Fahmida, I learned. She smelled like Tibet talcum powder and jasmine oil.

“I was your age when my mother dragged me into one of these places,” she winked at me. “I’ve been working at a parlour since.”

What fascinated me about Fahmida aunty was how much she owned her womanness. In a place where women walked with bent necks and crossed their arms around their chest in shame, Fahmida aunty didn’t mind wearing a kameez with a plunging neckline or bother to drape a dupatta to veil herself.

She didn’t mind caking her face with wrong shades of foundations and concealers that made her skin appear a shade lighter or loading mascara on her lashes, which made them look like chunky caterpillars. She laughed raucously and unapologetically, she walked with a swing in her hips, and slurped when she drank her chai.

She loved Amitabh Bachchan and played old Bollywood songs on her shiny speakers. She wore gaudy jewellery and bright hues and would often swear at the men from the window who’d try to take the parking space for her little scooter. She lived as if she belonged in the open, like a breath of fresh air, unlike the rest of us.

Women who visited her parlour, clad in burqas textured with cloying shame, would pare down to their lawn shalwar kameez upon seeing her face. Her face, which always gleamed like a burning candle, would melt away their carefully measured need to make themselves smaller. They would suddenly shed their idea of what’s dishonourable and conjure the confidence to bitch about their mothers and sisters-in-law in front of Fahmida aunty. That was her power. She would listen, really intently, and order sweet chai from the dhaba for you. She’d make you feel safe in her presence, with reassuring nods and glances, and have a totka for pretty much everything.

It filled me with disbelief, how modest women from the neighbourhood, marked and mottled with compliance, would let their hair down and talk so freely about their sex lives and men problems at Fahmida aunty’s beauty parlour. It was safe to be a woman because she wouldn’t look at you with questioning semaphoric eyebrows or make incondite remarks.

I started enjoying my monthly visits to the beauty parlour with my mother. There would be chai, laughter, gossip, and the magical unravelling of women whose minds and bodies never belonged to them.

When I turned twenty-three, my mother started complaining to Fahmida aunty about the strenuous process of finding me a man.

“She’s the eldest of my daughters, what am I to do?” My mother said. “Getting them married at the right age is important. If the eldest won’t marry, who’s going to marry the rest?”

“Nausheen, you’re being too hard on your daughter. The right man will come at the right time.” She looked at me and smiled, her dangly gold earrings hanging low on her lobes. “Have you got someone in mind already?”

I shook my head no and looked down, mortified. The task of finding a husband follows every girl in this place from the womb.

When my mother got up to leave after her fruity facial massage, her skin rubbed raw, and each hair plucked, I started following her before Fahmida aunty stopped me. She waited until my mother was out of earshot and pushed something inconspicuously into my palm.

“This is something that helped me meet my husband.” She whispered, her face serious.

I looked at what looked like an old, handmade beaded bracelet in confusion, the beads still warm from being held in her fist.

“I brought it from my village. Something my mother gave me, for a little good luck in the romantic department. It’s made with jade; it attracts good fortune and luck.”

I looked at her trusting face and smiled and thanked her. I put the bracelet inside my purse for safekeeping.

I didn’t wear the bracelet for a long time. I wasn’t ready for what it was to bring me yet. I let it sit in my bag for weeks, until it developed a voice of its own, always calling out to me. Always reminding me how I am running out of time.

-

It was the week leading up to Bakra Eid. The streets were lined with brown-eyed cows and goats with legs so skinny you’d think they’d collapse. I wondered if these animals were aware that they were fated for sacrifice. Their worth carefully calculated, from womb till they’re in front of a butcher’s knife. Their meat being prepared to be hung upside down to dry, and then rubbed with salt, yoghurt, and unripe papaya until it’s so tender it melts in your mouth.

The city would widen to make space for the sacrificial animals that would roam at night. Ropes tight around their necks, an end held by a herd of laughing children, bells jingling under the yellow streetlights, each mouth movement chewing dry grass that seemed like a prayer.

My mom and I visited the beauty parlour on chaand raat, the night before Eid, to get our hands painted with mehendi. We crossed the chai dhaba with its tin roof that was bending under the weight of the hanging branches of the ancient banyan tree, the cobbler with his rusty hammer and collection of leather scraps, the grocer batting flies, and the dimly lit dirt parking lot.

The two-roomed parlour was buzzing with activity in excitement for Eid. Two girls with thick braided hair, that looked like floppy ropes, sitting cross-legged on the floor, holding a cone of mehendi, making classic paisleys and coiling vines with their nimble fingers on soft white palms.

A flock of chatty women circled the mehendi girls, waiting for their turn, while the mehendi girls’ wrists coiled and twisted, painting concentric circles. My mom had brought over a small box of sweets for Fahmida aunty. It was to celebrate the finalisation of my wedding date.

When we spotted Fahmida aunty in the midst of all the chaand raat chaos, she didn’t look like her usual self. Her beautiful, luscious hair looked off-kilter, giving her a frazzled look. Her eyes were tired and looked sunken, the shadows under them like partial eclipses. Her skin, which always had that well-hydrated glow, appeared lacklustre, as if she were a crushed flower petal.

When her gaze finally fell on us, her mouth stretched into a bright smile. My mother handed her the box of sweets, and Fahmida aunty exploded with happiness upon the news. She held me in a hug, and I could smell the tangy sweat of toiling all day clinging to her lawn clothes. I spotted a concealed bruise on her face, hidden expertly under layers of creams and powders. I recoiled internally.

“Congratulations, beta!” she squealed. “Khuda tumhare naseeb achay karay. (May God grant you a good fate)”

She looked at me with wide eyes that disguised a dark omen. “Looks like the bracelet still works after all.”

She made me promise her that I would let her put mehendi on me on my wedding day.

We were walking back home when my mother started expressing concern over Fahmida’s husband beating her. “It’s getting out of hand now,” she said.

My pulse stuttered.

“Her husband has always been a chutiya.” My mother only swore like this on our trips to the beauty parlour. “Don’t ever bring it up in front of her.”

The sacrificial animals lined the streets, sitting with straw baskets filled with dry grass. They looked at me with deep, half-lidded eyes, as if they understood what I felt. It was like we knew what was coming for us. I removed the lucky bracelet immediately from my wrist and pushed it deep inside my handbag. It was suddenly starting to burn and pierce my skin.

It was Eid morning when I woke up with a headache. My soon-to-be husband was visiting to join my family for the festivities, and the entire house was busy making meetha and tucking everything into place.

I stood on the balcony and looked at the men gathering in the garden downstairs for the sacrifice. The animals stood next to the golden pothos, which covered the limewash walls. Each unfurling leaf appeared like a warning to me.

The air was thick and wet and smelled of monsoon soil and cow skin. I spotted my soon-to-be husband in the throng in his off-white kurta, his sleeves folded all the way to his elbows, not neatly, but hurriedly, and his curly pompadour making him stand out. They slipped a freshly sharpened butcher knife into his hands, and maybe it was just me, but his eyes looked almost lustful. They rolled the animal onto its back, four men holding its front and back hooves, exposing its soft neck. Its pearly skin gleamed in the sunlight as my soon-to-be husband slid the blade at the base of the throat. Not carefully, not cautiously, but with an erratic motion that made the animal tremble in helplessness.

Rosy flesh opened following the tip of the knife. I bit my tongue and tasted copper in my mouth. I could see into the eyes of the animal, now lolling in the aftershocks. I see his arms, coated in blood, and he looked almost satiated.

My Bethnal Green Queen

by Holly Sykes

She was just Sam when I knew her. From a single-parent family on a Bethnal Green estate in east London: humble origins. That’s what she says in her interviews. And it’s true. Her background was never desirable, not at the time, not when I knew her. Now that she’s made it, stories about growing up in a council block and stepping over comatose addicts in the concrete stairwell sound so, well, glamorous somehow. In that real-life, gritty kind of way. Like she’s walked off the set of a crime miniseries and straight into Hollywood.

I don’t suppose it can have been that easy, but Sam was the kind of person who made it look easy. As if poverty and despair simply washed over her. When I knew her, back when life was one never-ending party interrupted only by enough shifts at the restaurant to keep us in Mimosas, she was a porcelain queen with black hair that shone in the reflection of the retro glitterball. Now, on the posters, she looks just the same, but it’s hard to know how much of it's because of make-up and airbrushing.

It doesn’t matter how many times I see her God-sized head on the wall of a tube station or on the side of a bus, it still feels like I’ve bumped into my old friend. That moment of recognition. I can’t help it; my heart skips a beat. That’s the way she made me feel. Still makes me feel. Then I take in the two-dimensional nature of her. Up close, I can feel the ridges of the wall behind the poster. I can see the fibres of paper that weave together. Printed and scaled up, she’s a representation of someone I once knew. She’s no longer just Sam. She’s Samantha Burnett.

At school, she was popular with students and teachers alike. Later, when we were working at Papagallos, she had that effect on the customers and waiters. Even Papa Gallo himself, who wasn’t Italian, but a Bosnian survivor of the 90s war, was soft on her. He’d feed her delicate quantities of ravioli as if she were a cat. Sometimes he’d sneak her a nip of limoncello, and have one himself, if the day’s takings had been satisfactory. No one else was offered this small bonus, but there was an unspoken agreement that Sam was the best of us. She earned most of the tips after all, and insisted on sharing them equally, as if she didn’t rely on tips at all. As if Papa’s pay was enough to cover London rent in the new millennium. Sam didn’t have an easy life, but you wouldn’t know it; she made living look effortless and that’s a very attractive quality.

It shouldn’t have been a surprise to me that she’d make it. She always seemed to be surrounded by people of that world when we were out on the town: minor celebs, footballers from the lower leagues, would-be rappers. I recognised them from the magazines and was quite silly over them, but she treated them as normal people and they seemed to like that. They didn’t know she returned home every night to the council estate.

When a repeat customer at the restaurant finally got her to agree to a date, I didn’t think it would go anywhere. Neil Bampton claimed to be a barrister working at Lincoln’s Inn, and he would sweep into the restaurant demanding his ‘usual table and a bottle of your finest Chianti Classico.’ The way he pronounced Classico with a faux Italian accent was enough to make me want to throw up, but his slicked hair and pointy shoes didn’t help matters. He was obnoxious, but he was persistent, and Sam was kind. I’ve often wondered how many acts of kindness have led to a woman’s downfall. But I didn’t worry about Sam. Like I said, danger always washed over her, and it would be no different this time.

Neil planned to take her to the opera because she was ‘a classy bird.’ There were so many things wrong with that sentiment that I didn’t know where to start, and that’s probably where I went wrong. I should have said more, but I had so many objections, I couldn’t get them straight in my head. There was also the small matter of Sam being far more experienced than me in the whole relationship game. My boyfriend was a sweet young man who was still at college. He made a daily packed lunch for himself and generally thought the best of people.

From this sweet little bubble of loveliness, I did not feel equipped to weigh in on Sam’s romantic choices.

So, Neil took her to see Cosi Fan Tutte in Covent Garden. ‘All women are the same,’ he said, winking at me as he helped Sam on with her coat. He was trying to get one over on me, but I looked it up later. Cosi fan tutte: all women are like that. I kissed Sam on the cheek and wished her a good time.

We weren’t on the same shift for another week, so it was days later that I discovered she was going out with him again, this time to the ballet. When I asked her if she actually liked the opera and the ballet, she shrugged as if that wasn’t relevant. Did she like Neil? I asked. Again, she shrugged. She was still forming an opinion I thought, although how anyone couldn’t see he was completely detestable was beyond me. Unlike me, her head had never been turned by the many glitterati we’d met while clubbing, so perhaps there was something about Neil that I’d missed.

The ballet was Swan Lake, which I’d always thought a gruesome story for children at Christmas, not least for a romantic date, ending as it does with a double suicide, but again Sam came away having enjoyed herself enough to allow Neil to progress to a weekend in the country. ‘Jealous, are you, little black swan?’ he said to me, a sneer on his lips. Sam wasn’t party to any of this, of course. She was in the loo at the back of the restaurant doing her hair before Neil whisked her away to the Cotswolds.

I don’t know exactly what happened on that weekend away, but afterwards I didn’t see a whole lot of Sam. Papa went around with a thundercloud over his head. Rumour was that she’d picked up shifts in another restaurant, somewhere more central, upmarket, where the punters were paid members, and the tips were eye-popping. Neil would come looking for her, drunk, but Papa would chase him off. Seems like Sam had slipped free from the slimeball’s grip. It made me wonder whether she’d always known he wasn’t good enough for her.

Don’t ask me why I didn’t tell him to clear off when he found me sitting on the back steps of the restaurant taking a cigarette break. It was a balmy June evening, one of those really golden moments in London when you feel it heating up for the summer, when the hanging baskets are spilling with colour, and music drifts from open windows. The kind of evening that has you yearning for something, someone.

Neil looked glum, and I gave him my cigarette. He asked where Sam was, and I could truthfully answer that I had no idea. Every once in a while, I’d caught a glimpse of her dancing in a nightclub, but by that stage she was spending most of her time in the VIP section, or so I’d heard. Some might think of her desertion as a betrayal, but I never held it against her. She was a free spirit, and I wasn’t going to be the one to cage her.

I let Neil sound off a little – I could smell beer on his breath – then I suggested he should accept that she’d moved on. He cried at that, and his head found my shoulder. I thought him pathetic, but I was lonely – my sweet boyfriend had exited the scene some time before - and it was nice to feel needed, even if only for ten minutes or so. My shift was due to end soon, and he said he’d wait then we could go for a drink. I didn’t make him any promises, but he was still on the steps when I emerged with my bag and paltry tips that wouldn’t even cover my fare home. We went to the bar next door.

He'd wanted to photograph Sam, but she hadn’t let him. Now that she was gone, he didn’t even have a photo by which to remember her. Those were the days before social media. I was about the same height and size as her, maybe I’d let him photograph me instead? His words came back to me: ‘All women are the same.’

‘I’ve got brown hair,’ I said. ‘My eyes are smaller, my nose is longer.’

‘Tie your hair up,’ was his answer. ‘Turn your face away.’

I’d had several tequila shots, but I wasn’t too drunk not to know what I was doing. At his flat, I stripped down and let him arrange me, his cold fingers prodding me this way and that.

In one or two of the photos, I suppose I could have been mistaken for Sam – he had a talent for manipulating light – but mostly it was in his imagination. I allowed him his photos, then we slept together. You might think it was upsetting that he called me Sam throughout, but it was hardly a surprise. All evening he’d treated me like a stand-in. Call me crazy, but it was quite a thrill to be her that night. In a way, I felt it brought us closer together, me and Sam. I’d missed her, you see.

Occasionally I wonder if Neil ever sent my photo to the restaurant like he threatened to if I didn’t sleep with him again, but I left Papagallos not long after. If he did, old Papa will have had a great laugh over it. That makes me smile. I don’t exactly have the body of Samantha Burnett, but Papa had seen far worse sights in his time. Bodies, naked or otherwise, strewn about the streets of Sarajevo.

It’s not only posters of Sam on the tube that take me by surprise. It’s all the promotional stuff she does when one of her movies is about to come out. I’ll be scrolling through reels and suddenly there she is with her flawless face and easy charm. Moments like that, I wonder how differently things might have played out if Neil had taken naked photographs of her instead of me. How much money he could have made blackmailing her, how much power he could have had over her life. Whenever I watch one of her films, I think of Neil watching her too, jealous and embittered. He took photographs of me because he couldn’t take them of Sam. How that must enrage him. I suppose he could get a little something out of me, post my photos on the internet, but I had to reinvent myself when I entered the corporate world and I go by a different name now, so he would struggle to find me. In any case, I’m not sure anyone would care. I simply don’t have the currency of Samantha Burnett.

I do love watching her films. She’s so talented. In those films, she’s real. More real to me than I am to myself. My Bethnal Green Queen. When I’m watching her in a scene, she has the ability to draw me right in. She makes me feel as if I’m there with her, crying at the deathbed of her child or racing through trees to escape an axe-murderer. I feel her quiet devastation, her terror, her courage, her vitality, and it’s easy to forget myself.

Old Gold

by Penny Wei

Before San Francisco was 三藩市 (San-fan-city), it was 旧金山 (Old-gold-mountain), a city that prospered in its mythical pledge of fortune. Gold to humans were as flame to moths. One sniff of a probability to upward social mobility and citizens would abandon their mundane, repetitive lives in pursuit of a quicker, richer and more dazzling trajectory to success.

When Ba abandoned Ma and the 3-monthed fetus in her womb, she called him a 负心汉 – a callous, unfaithful betrayer who chased after a bone with glittering clings of flesh like a dog. Ba knelt on the floor and grabbed Ma’s waist, piously kissing her lumping belly, again and again. He stayed there long enough for his knees to numb, long enough for the floorboards and the mother-skin to remember the shape of his organs. Ma did not touch his hair. She watched the place where his mouth met her skin and watched the reverence gather and thin, gather and thin, like breath on glass.

When he rose, his eyes smudged everything but the contours of his wife. In attempts to squeeze out salty water, he reprinted in his mind the last image of his lover – a bony, short-haired woman blemished by the blood and scales of naughty fish.

“When I return for you and our third child, I will bring for us endless money and an American passport. Money is all we need now. I will change our lives. Just you wait and I will prove it to you. Just you wait.”

People said that there were three females in the house, but only two were women. Ma was the woman who loved Ba with a devotion soft as water and wavy as willow, swallowing crusty-sweet peach pits in her pupils as she let the broke fisherman impregnate her three times and marry her ring-less. My sister Xin was the woman who woke before the roosters, rinsed the rice twice, once for grit and once for care, cut scallions thinner than necessary, and stored Ma’s sentences the way others stored coins.

So when Ba chose one person to accompany his journey, the second daughter, I, Yu, was the one and only choice. Yu (余) meant remnants and sounded like the character Yu (鱼), which meant fish. From the start I was a child thriving on remnants of a dish and entertained myself with the agility of a leaping fish — a headfast, rocky creature often disliked for my mischief and cherished for my quick-wit.

Before heading off to the deck, Ma sat me down and shaved my head bald to, one, prevent the upcoming filth to bestow my hair with lice and, two, let me become rootless. Circling my forehead, her fingers lingered as if they were the softest thing that will ever touch me from now on.

We set sail in August, summer, when the village was still loud with life. Nets dried on bamboo poles, silvered with fish scales and sun. The river smelled thick and sweet, and the paddies held the sky so fully it seemed the earth might drown in blue. It should have been a season for staying.

In the night, the deck was pressed flat by bodies and breath. Men slept on planks rubbed smooth by salt and fear, turning in turns when space ran out. I was almost dreaming when I heard Ba whisper his confessions, the quiet, vulnerable type men would utter in sorrow to their wives to justify an affair. Much of the stuff I already knew: storms had begun to speak early that year and debts did not wait for weather. It was a choice out of necessity. And that he made the right choice.

An under-loved, inattentive daughter to a woeful father was as a lousy, playful cat to its owner. My head nodded with the currents water along with some “嗯s” and “哦s” as to prove the loyalty of my wind-drifted ear. I was not a big fan of the redundant chewings of his introspections, how his long-made decision was first flawless, then a mistake, and then somewhat amendable, how his fidelity to the big-bellied lady awaiting on the shore stuck strong, and then wavered, and then meandered back again. I told him there was no need to fret already: a decision made is a pot of cabbage stew spilled. No matter what there is no reverse. He nodded, and reassured me that this departure was only temporary. Fishermen always leave and return. This was only a longer tide.

Days ahead were measured in meals. Food always came at the tipping point of hunger, and water, however much algae and rust it carried, counted mouthful to mouthful. The ship moved on without days. Six weeks passed. Then eight. Then time loosened its grip, and the crossing became the only place that existed. I was blessed with an American dream to keep me aloof from boredom. I often asked Ba and other men on deck what America was like– whether gold mines sprawled as vines and opportunity scattered as redbeans, awaiting for us to scoop.

A man with a scarred left eye told me that America was a place where a man could outrun his past, if he ran fast enough.

I told him that I wasn’t a fast runner, but I was a fast swimmer. Does that still count?

When America almost reached my feet, I reached for my head. The stubble had come back uneven, as if it could not agree with itself. It had a soft shadow in some places, a bare shine in others, and small stubborn tufts rising where the blade had missed and a mother’s mercy had intervened. I pressed my palm against it, feeling heat and the faint rasp of new insistence, and for a moment I did not know whether I was growing or being stripped again. The wind of the bay found sport in tampering the skin I have yet to learn how to protect, lifting nothing and braiding nothing.

One thing about my hair was that I used to have lots of it. It reached past my waist, heavy with the smelling of soggy, old nets. When I fished with Ba, I tied it with twine torn from a rice sack, looping it twice so it wouldn’t slip, then tucked the knot under my collar. Sometimes when I forgot it would drag. It either caught a hook or brushed the slime-slick flank of a carp, pulling in alongside the dynamic of the line. When the wind lifted my hair I had to stop, teeth working through the snags, spit and fingers doing what combs couldn’t. To me, being a woman for the past eighteen years didn’t go past anything but a flick of such a burden the way Ba flicked water from nets. At dawn, it held the cold. By noon, it held the sun. And after hours by the water, it stiffened with salt.

Boys were different. Their braids were a little skinnier, with a shaved front and a long queue at the back. The boy that sailed with us, Han, whom I have managed to befriend, had a braid so skinny like a single spike of a wheat. When he held it in his clothes I could barely notice, so oftentimes we looked the same, with no particular disparities in height, strength and figure. We were both undernourished in our own ways: mines leaving me breast-less and his leaving him hollow-cheeked, all angles and tendons. His shoulders jutted like the corners of a crate while mine sloped inward, taught early to make room. Sun flattened us both. We shared the same cough at night, the same dizziness when we stood too fast.

He asked me if I would ever be able to breastfeed a baby, as at that time I was flat as a bamboo stick, so I told him I am starting to become one of them. He then told me if I were to become a man, I would’ve been executed now for my hair not obliging to the laws of the Manchus.

So when I first stepped on American lands I thought to myself: they were all gonna be executed by the Manchus.

Upon the shore, Americans gathered, met and walked. American boys had short, golden hair, as though I would’ve assumed from the Westerners I’ve seen before who conglomerated in foreign factories throughout the Riverfront trading zones, smoking from pipes and laughing from lungs.

The men here acted similarly, except it was more of an outspread than a sole gathering of them. Their voices came first. Loud and loose, tossed into the air without care for where they landed. Laughter broke open like wood split too hard. Words struck each other and scattered. I could not understand them, but somewhat understood the rhythm of impatience.

I wasn’t able to go any closer to the noisy, white men, all of which had hair with the resemblance of a lion’s fur. But male lions had long fur, so white men were like female lions, and white women were like male lions. I was drawn to them for their delicacy, that is, a type of cleanliness and steadiness that varied from my own. But of course I was not of remorse: I have been on sea and unwashed for ages, of course I was unkempt in any form!

In Guangdong, foreigners were something brighter, esteemed with their freckles that dotted them elegance and a curled tongue none of us understood. For a moment, a sudden lightning of fear struck me: never, or at least for the many months ahead, will I ever get to hear Cantonese from anyone’s mouth other than my dear chinese acquaintances! Who will be there to understand me?

When I spoke of my concern with Ba, he only laughed and waved his hand. Oh no, baby, there are more Chinese acquaintances here than you can think of. We are not alone!

Oh but how would one define loneliness? Ba, we are but flies huddling and buzzing at a trash can, eagerly and hostilely battling for a bite of shit. We are alone in that we are in absence of a united heart. The heart has long been segregated since the two-month fetus and many fetuses are left father-less or waterborne. Where can unity be found in a land different from my own, where I am nothing but an alien, estranged in conversations, cloth and hair color?

When I looked at Ba, he seemed way too enthusiastic, as for gold to overtake his eyes, bubbling with an ambition for a near-prosperous future and posterity of wealth. So I only asked him about their hair color, and why Americans had yellow hair. Unsurprisingly, he told me that Americans were wealthy enough to brush gold onto their hair, and thus the dazzling hue. One day, we will brush gold onto our strands of hair as well, as to cover the present ebony. Soon enough, our whole bodies will shine golden.

Our conversation and my yet-to-come insult was interrupted on a pat on Ba’s back. Precisely speaking, it was the sound of footsteps that came seconds before that — the sound of leather stomping against rocky grounds — the sound of money. I have heard that soft, crunchy sound when I worked as a bootblack for rich merchants who travelled to Guangdong for business. Some men were nice and gave me a few copper coins along with encouragement that let me scrub faster. The others lifted up my chin with the tip of their shoes, telling me: poor little ghost, this might be the only leather you’ll ever get to touch in your life! Might as well appreciate it and scrub cleaner! Oftentimes I wouldn’t know how to respond and only let the warmth of the worn-leather penetrate my head, hoping that the same leather wouldn’t leave a bruise or stain on me today.

When I turned around, I was met with a Chinese man, one like myself, but with short hair. His hands, dry and practiced, closed around Ba’s sleeve, with a grip of someone used to counting bodies the way others counted crates.

“You from China?”

As if he wasn’t himself.

“Yes, yes, both me and her, and him.” Ba pointed at Han and I.

“Okay,” He nodded. “You three, follow the crowd over there.” He pointed east, at a bustling stampede of people.

“Okay, okay.” Ba answered semi-obediently, eyes dropping to his leather shoes, then drifting upwards to his denim pants and linen shirt. All expensive, foreign clothing.

We were not allowed to drift. Men in dark cotton jackets began to funnel us decisively away from the open shore and its shouting brightness. Their Cantonese was clipped and economical, the kind used for ledgers. Names were asked. Then villages were named. Then districts were sorted. A voice called “Toisan,” another “Zhongshan,” another “Panyu,” and bodies answered by moving, as iron filings drawn to separate magnets. Ba spoke for us. He always did. I stood half a step behind him, trained by years of nets and debt to know where daughters belonged. We were pressed through streets that narrowed quickly, and the city itself learned to hold its breath around us. Buildings leaned in. Signs stacked vertically, characters painted thick and confident, promising services we did not yet understand: lodging, employment, remittance, mediation, burial. The smell shifted from salt and tar to soy, damp wood, old oil. I recognized it as the smell of men waiting.

Inside, the huiguan was darker than I expected. Practical-dark. Windows were high and light was rationed. A long table ran the center of the room, scarred with years of elbows and ink spills. Ledgers lay open like animals pinned for study.

Ba gave his name. His village. The name of his father. The man across from him nodded, dipped his brush, and wrote as if the information had been waiting all along. Numbers followed. Passage cost. Interest. Advance already eaten by time. I watched Ba’s shoulders tighten as his future was converted into columns.

When the man looked up, his eyes flicked to me.

“How old?”

“十八,” Ba said, after a pause.

I looked back at him. I was in fact, not yet eighteen.

But age is just a number, right?

The man looked at me again.

“Boy or girl?”

“Girl.”

This time I answered before Ba could.

“Aiya, girl? Girl not good. Do less labor earn less money.”

“She can do hard work. She fished with me back in China. Hands and back of rough steel. Treat her like a boy.”

I looked at Ba again. Really, I couldn’t see much in his eyes, despite how shallow they already were. A man who can abandon his wife and two children for gold will treat his daughter like a dog. Ma was right. What much can we expect from a callous, unfaithful betrayer?

We were led upstairs to a boarding room. The stairs were steep and smelled of boiled greens and unwashed cloth. The room itself was long and narrow, lined with sleeping platforms like shelves. Men lay already, some awake, some sunk into the kind of sleep that arrives only after surrender, drooling and murmuring. At least I hope they have a nice dream.

Ba and I were given a corner, a mat, a bucket and a bowl each.

“This is temporary,” the attendant said, not unkindly, and I believed him because nothing here pretended to be permanent.

That night, I listened.

Men whispered of contracts already arranged. Of camps upriver where gold had been scraped thin but not clean. Of white foremen who paid late and counted wrong. Of others — Chinese men — who spoke the same dialect but wore better shoes and grew softer faces. Contractors. Middlemen. They came and went through the huiguan like owners inspecting stock.

They looked like us, if you squinted. Same bones. Same mouths. But when they spoke, their words landed aloof.

One of them looked at Ba and smiled in recognition.

“You’ll go north,” he said. “Three months. Maybe more. Wages docked for passage. Half sent home.”

Ba nodded. He nodded a lot now. It was a new habit, acquired somewhere between the sea and the ledger.

Like father, like daughter, they say. I smile, and nod along, agreeing to the instability that lay before me, actively succumbing to the passiveness of life.

-

Afterall, I was still, biologically, classified as a woman. Hence, I was sent near a Chinese railroad spur labor group, where I was taught to wash their clothes and cook rice, fish and some other simple stew — some conventional labor not unfamiliar to my hands. Ba worked in railroad labor but a few districts away. When I asked him why he still had to work and not mine gold by himself he told me how we practically were still in debt. The voyage from home to here was not free (when he arrived at this sentence, he stopped to mock my naivety, of which I fully take the blame). A steerage ticket from Guangdong to San Francisco costs several taels of silver, which vastly exceeded our budget as a family who lived on copper cash. Very few households held spare silver, while we barely had none. His eyes, when attempting to search for the right words to embellish his deeds, were ingenuously guiltless, as if a fresh-born fawn trembling its legs. I was surprised at his sheer audacity, and admired his courage to tell me undisguised. He then introduced me to the credit-ticket system, at which most migrants travelled under. We didn’t have money to pay for the full trip so instead, huiguan affiliate paid for the passage upfront.

At the end of his early-morning speech I smelt his breath (or maybe my own) that smelt like a starved stomach and its grueling, howling acid that stirred within it, and understood three, simple things.

One, we arrived owing tons of money. Two, our bodies were sheer collaterals. Three, freedom, and, the great dream of gold, were things that only came after repayment.

My first day of work began before the sun remembered itself. I sat up into a cold that felt already used, as if the air had passed through too many lungs before reaching mine. Around me, men were rising in pieces: a cough first, then a spine, then a curse swallowed back into habit. Outside, the camp lay low and gray. Canvas tents sagged like tired mouths. Smoke from last night’s fires still clung to the ground, thin and sour. Somewhere upriver, metal rang against stone – one man starting early, or late, or never stopping. Time here did not seem to move forward.

A big, iron pot was shoved into my hands. It was still wet from the river and marred from leftover food stains.

“Rice,” a man said, pointing, as if I had ever cooked anything else.

I crouched by the fire pit and learned quickly how hunger sharpened everything. The rice was poor with broken grains and dull with age, but when it hit the water it bloomed, white spreading like a small mercy. I stirred with a stick, wrist aching a little from poor rest, long travel and the sleeping position last night. To extent Ba was wrong — I was vulnerable, just like anyone else.

The only thing that differed was a heart to show my capability and to show that I belonged. I was never a good fisherman, and yet I craved to become a fisherman’s daughter of good competence. Although I couldn’t really define myself now. What am I? The daughter of an immigrant, child of a railroad laborer, and hence a cook. I stared at the men that stood before me and took into account their cracked skin, chapped lips, and the small streams of blood that spotted their face in one corner and another. The smoke of the pot stung my eyes.

I sighed.

I guess I will see myself as the sustainer for the survival of these poor, poor people.

My colleagues were a lot of other Chinese women with differing dialects. Most of them were wives of the toiling men.

Having worked at the railroad camp for a few hours, I was surprised by how little white faces I saw and how little English I heard. English was something too distant to labor. Seldom a few American workers would pass and stare at us as apes – a dirty, yellow crowd of animals crassly moaning foreign languages. Meeting their eyes I only beamed at how Xin wasn’t the one here, with her and her heart soft as spring snow.

When the men lined up, they did not look at me. They looked at the pot.

Bowls appeared. Tin mismatched. Hands thrust forward. I learned to portion fast, and tried not to be generous nor stingy. Too much and someone else complained. Too little and someone remembered your face. I did not know yet which was worse.

After breakfast came washing.

The river was colder than the sea had ever been. Snowmelt, a woman said, laughing as if pain were a joke you told yourself to survive. Shirts and trousers were stiff with sweat, dirt ground into the seams. I beat them against rocks slick with algae, the sound dull and endless. My fingers went numb first, then burned, then disappeared entirely.

鱼儿鱼儿水中游，

游来游去乐悠悠

Little fish, little fish, roaming in the water;

going nowhere and everywhere, just glad to go.

Deaf Connections

by Paul Hostovsky

The Deaf guy I’m interpreting for today at the ophthalmologist’s office isn’t exactly a friend. But I know him. He’s not a family member either, though I know his whole family. “Are you a family member or just a friend?” the doctor asks me as I follow him and the Deaf patient into the examining room. “Neither,” I reply. “I’ve been hired by your office to interpret for you and your patient—I do this for a living.” He gives me a surprised look as though he’d never seen anything like it in the history of ophthalmology.

Doctors can be pretty dumb about Deaf people and sign language. But the thing is—and I don’t tell the doctor this—not only do I know this Deaf guy and his family, I have interpreted for all of them at one time or another; I have interpreted for his Deaf wife; I have interpreted for his Deaf son and his Deaf son’s Deaf ex and their Deaf children, i.e. this Deaf guy’s grandchildren. As a matter of fact, just last month the ex and her Deaf father had dinner with me and my Deaf wife at my Deaf sister-in-law’s house—along with some other Deaf friends whose kids and exes and step kids I know and have occasionally interpreted for. So, you see, it’s all kind of incestuous.

Which is why the National Registry of Interpreters for the Deaf has a strict Code of Professional Conduct that we sign language interpreters must abide by, and it includes, among other tenets, the one about all assignment-related information being kept strictly confidential. We can’t talk about our interpreting work outside of the assignment, and we can’t tell anyone anything about the Deaf consumers that might identify them.

So you’re probably wondering right about now if I’ve broken the Code of Professional Conduct by telling you about this Deaf guy I’m interpreting for at the ophthalmologist’s office today, not to mention having mentioned his entire Deaf family. I wondered about that myself as I began writing this little essay. But the thing is, nothing I have told you so far about him or his family would allow you to identify them. There are thousands of Deaf people who go to the ophthalmologist and who have Deaf families: Deaf wives, Deaf children, Deaf exes, Deaf grandchildren. This Deaf guy could be anybody. He could be any Deaf body.

I like writing about Deaf people because I have spent the last forty years learning their language and culture and hanging out with them and falling in love with them and, of course, interpreting for them. There’s a lot I could tell you about them but since I don’t want to violate the Code of Professional Conduct, and since I don’t want to be accused of speaking for Deaf people—who are perfectly capable of speaking for themselves—and since I don’t want to be one of those annoying hearing people who goes around spouting off about Deaf people as if he actually knew what it’s like to be Deaf—I don’t know what it’s like to be Deaf, as my Deaf wife is only too happy to remind me—I should probably quit while I’m ahead.

But let me just say this.

Sometimes I wish I were Deaf. Because as a hearing person who is in love with Deaf people and their language and culture, I will always be on the outside looking in. I will never quite belong, not the way Deaf people belong among each other. Which is not to say that all Deaf people feel a sense of belonging among all other Deaf people. Far from it. They are as heterogeneous as any other group of people and there are circles and cliques and groups and tribes and communities within the so-called Deaf community.

But you can study a foreign spoken language, move to a country where that language is spoken, grow fluent in the language and culture of that country, get a visa, get a green card, get naturalized, even marry someone from that country and become a citizen of that country. But there is no country of the Deaf. And there is no Deaf citizenship for hearing people. But we can be allies.

I like to think of myself as an ally, but I’m also a voyeur. A fly on the wall. I mean sometimes it feels like I’m watching a soap opera. Lots of soap operas. The Deaf soaps. This one married that one, had an affair with this other one who used to be a hopeless drunk but is sober now thanks to AA. That one is dating this one who is recently divorced from that one who is a famous Deaf person—famous in the Deaf world—and is in a rebound relationship with that one who is estranged from their kids. This one has a terminal illness and that one just got out of jail and this one is probably headed for jail. This one is almost blind but refuses to sign tactilely and that one recently had all their teeth extracted and this one had bariatric surgery and that one was a phenomenal basketball player in high school but is now a drug addict. This one has a Ph.D. and that one has HIV and this one has an ankle bracelet and that one is an artist and this one is a carpenter and can fix anything and that one is a financial planner and is richer than Croesus. I mean I could write a book. I mean sometimes it feels like I’m living vicariously, watching all these dramas play out from the cool remove of the sign language interpreter—the fly on the wall, who must remain neutral and confidential and professional in the midst of all this Deaf buzz.

And now the eye doctor, who may know a thing or two about ophthalmology but is as dumb as they come about Deaf people and sign language, looks at me and asks me to ask this Deaf guy—who isn’t exactly my friend, though I know him—if he’s experiencing any flashes or floaters. And the Deaf guy notices, notices that this eye doctor won’t look him in the eye—doesn’t see him—and says to the doctor to ask him himself, in the second person. But the doctor doesn’t know what the second person is. And the doc looks at me again and asks me what the Deaf guy means by that. And I am tempted, so tempted, to reach across and gingerly take hold of the doc’s chin, and gently turn his head clockwise until he’s facing the Deaf guy—looking at the Deaf guy, eye to eye—and prompt him: “Now ask again.”

Just Pick One

by Jane Ann Valentine

I am fifteen, waiting with Pop for our number to blink red on the crooked black monitor at the DMV in Ladson, South Carolina.

Ladson was bursting at the seams. Bulldozers and paving machines were as common as sweet tea and porch swings. Overnight, our house number jumped from three digits to four, though the paint color changed only once a decade. Most people don’t realize that where we live isn’t a city or a town—just an unincorporated community without so much as a welcome sign. Pride lifts Pop’s chin, puffing up his soft chest that sinks into the swell of a Santa-sized beer belly. A grease-stained wife-beater stretches tight beneath dark denim overalls. He’s taken a break from tinkering in our garage to escort me two turns and one stoplight to this rite of passage. He’s been through this three times: once for himself back when TV was just black-and-white; once with my mother, when he decided she should work outside the home; and once with my brother on his fifteenth birthday. They passed everyone back in Pop’s day. Mom passed on the first attempt—the instructor had asked her out despite the wedding ring. But it had taken my brother three tries to pass, and it was a sore subject.

Pop had quit on them. With Mom, it was the language barrier. When she mixed up her lefts and rights, her stops and starts, Pop’s lightning-quick temper flared, and Kapampangan curses fell from her mouth like tiny knives wrapped in silk. She took to calling Pop Kulo, which means to boil. He laughed at the endearment, but she smiled knowingly. With Rob, it was his slow-as-molasses reactions. Pop dropped them each off unceremoniously at Rusty’s Driving School, with a hollered “Good luck” out the window.

I was his last chance to get it right. And I needed to pass the first time. For both our bragging rights.

Our number flashes red, and Pop tailgates me to the counter.

“Congratulations, darlin’,” the clerk says, smacking her gum, mouth wide open. I see more crown than tooth. I smell artificial mint jockeying with stale coffee and cigarettes.

He beams, inspecting the driver’s permit form over my shoulder, and my No. 2 pencil shakes across the page. Until.

Race.

It’s the only question I didn’t study for. Three bubbles stare back at me—Black, white, Other.

Pop studies my hand as it hovers between white and Other. He looks at the fidgeting line of teens forming behind us.

“White,” he states, like it’s obvious.

I bristle and whisper that “Mom is Filipina. So I am at least half not-white.”

He looks around the room like a bomb is about to go off. His face is redder than usual, and his eyes grow almost wider than the bifocals that magnify them.

“Excuse me,” I say to the clerk, who has already gone back to her Solitaire game. “Can I fill in more than one bubble?”

“Nope. Sorry, sweetheart.” More mint. More stale coffee and cigarettes. “Messes up the machine.”

“But I’m not just one race.”

She turns from the bulky monitor and looks at me—takes in my dark hair, almond-shaped eyes, and small flat button nose—then the white man towering behind me, his silvering blonde hair, ruddy complexion, and patrician nose. Her face flickers with surprise, then confusion. Pop and I exchange looks and hold our breath.

Then she looks back toward her monitor and shrugs. “Just pick one.”

The line behind me coils around the room and hugs the back wall. Heads pop out to see what the holdup is. My cheeks blaze, and my shirt clings to the sweat on my back as I glance at the line, a gigantic serpent with many heads, all judging me.

The holdup is an existential crisis, but to them, I’m just slow with a pencil.

Pop’s hand lands heavy and damp on my shoulder. “You’re wh-hite,” he says, slow and deliberate, like the word is more than one syllable.

Would loyalty to him betray my mother, along with her family back in the barrio—the ones we send money orders to and letters on cassette tapes, twice a month?

Sweat slicks my palms, and the pencil slides loose. The tip splinters as I darken the bubble for Other and try to hand it over before he can see. But he’s several heads taller, and his face tells me I’ve failed—in more ways than one.

He walks resolutely out to the car, and even though I was supposed to drive us home, he is buckled in and turning the key before I open the passenger door.

On the radio, Patsy Cline is already singing about “Walkin’ After Midnight” and “sayin’ I love you,” like it just might be my romantic future and not the nightmare I am contemplating. He swerves onto the busy road. I grip my thumb tight inside my fist—a little trick that calms me when Pop drives too fast or too slow, always on the lookout for wildlife rather than other cars. I can never tell if he’s being careful or careless.

We jerk into our circle driveway when he breaks the silence with a heavy sigh.

“Other. Is that really how you see yourself?”

My eyes water and my throat burns. I stare at the smudge his thumb left on the steering wheel and let the answer die there.

He looks older than when we left the house. I notice white creeping into his whiskers and sideburns.

He turns off the ignition and slams the car door.

A month earlier, at the kitchen table, I had asked him how he’d feel if I liked Henry—a sweet, bespectacled Black boy who offered to carry my books and passed me thick love letters folded into paper footballs in homeroom. My father had set his sweaty tumbler of sweet tea down without a coaster, and I bit my lip. He crossed his thick, freckled arms above the heft of his belly and whistled, taking his own sweet time.

“How would I feel? Well, I wouldn’t like it.” His lips stayed puckered like he was about to say something else.

The color left my face.

“I’d be disappointed, to tell you the truth, but I’m not sure if I can explain why,” he said quietly.

He must have seen the word inside my head, traveling toward my own pursed lips: Why.

“What if—what if Rob—if he brought home a girl…who was Black?” I asked, tripping over my words. “Like Whitney?”

He turned toward me, recognition in his eyes, and licked his thin, dry lips.

“Well, that would be fine, I suppose.”

“But not fine for me. To be with a Black boy. Even if he was light-skinned?” I taunted, shaking every word like a gift I was shaking to guess its contents.

“I think I’d see it…as a rejection of me. Don’t little girls want to marry someone in the image of their father?”

“Who’s talking about marriage? I just said what if I liked him?”

“And I gave you my answer.”

When Henry stood too close in the hallway, my stomach fluttered—but Pop’s voice echoed in my head, steady as a caution sign.

A year later I brought a boy home for dinner. Wesley. I was smitten. He was half-white and half–West Indian, with dark chocolate skin and wavy, coarse hair, funny and handsome in an offbeat way, his cheekbones pitched so sharply they made him look like a chipmunk when he smiled.

My parents loved him immediately. I was surprised by my father’s ease. When Wesley and I later broke up, Pop seemed more heartbroken than I was. I asked once if he had ever minded that Wesley was Black. He stopped, genuinely puzzled.

“I guess it doesn’t matter after all,” he said.

Later, the DMV form came back to me—the bubble for Other faintly smeared.

I had mistaken being marked for being seen.

From the kitchen, my mother’s Filipino lilt moved through the house.

I belonged.

A Good Cheese

by Nathan Cover

Every day I’d start my journey the same way. I’d take the tiny elevator down the six floors to the bottom. Close the interior doors first, otherwise the elevator wouldn’t move. I’d go down with Serban or Andrei if they had an early class at the university. My host family mother wanted them to spend as much time with me as possible. I was supposed to be helping them with their English. I’d talk to them in my shitty, pidgin Romanian and they’d laugh until they cried and answer me in English. They looked up to me. It was kind of sad. They thought I was a good guy, here to help out the poor Romanians. They couldn’t tell yet that I was a loser, another drunk running away from America. We’d get out on the bottom floor. Walk through the markets that were clustered around the bottom of the bloc like a bruise stemming out from a black eye in shades of yellow and ghastly purple. They sold shirts there out of bags. All my clothes were Romanian by this point. The American clothes I’d come with had evaporated from overuse.

I parted ways with Serban and Andrei and walked down to the train station. There was a long bridge that went over the station, which swung high up in the air and looked very unstable. There were missing planks of wood, but because I took it every day I knew where to step and where not to. I’d look down at the trains underneath, mostly World War Two relics, and occasionally an Intercity, the modern ones. After the train station it was all uphill, a good half hour walk from my host family’s flat to the school. During the school year it was nice, despite the gray of the air, the shitty Novemberness of everything. It was the same gray Midwestern air with no sunlight poking through, the same shitty November feelings I used to have at home but now translated romantic and majestical. It was only nice because during the school year there would also be college girls walking up the hill of Copou with their books. They were tiny girls and they carried their books with their arms across their chest, like it was still the 1950s. And they were dressed in heels like it meant something to be a person who went to class. The road up the hill of Copou was lined with lime trees and it gave the air a fragrance. Sometimes I’d buy a loaf of bread to snack on throughout the day for seven lei from ladies at the bread stand, always stoic and unsmiling. I’d lay my coins on the counter in exact change and they would hand me a bread with no words passed between us.

Today I’d have the fifth graders make a threefold travel brochure. For the older students we’d discuss pages from a Lonely Planet guide about their country and I’d ask them how did it feel? It usually made them angry. They wanted me to know that Romania wasn’t all gypsies and drunks. I already knew that, but it got them talking. It’s hard to conjugate verbs when you’re angry, but that’s how you get better at speaking. I wasn’t that much older than some of the girls in the twelfth-grade classes I taught, but I tried to be professional, between occasional glances at their cleavage.

They didn’t have lunch at school. So the school day ended earlier and everyone went home to have lunch to save money. Since I was making a big American salary of $300 a month. I went to the cantina at the university. I would get a ciorba radauteana, a creamy chicken soup with pools of fat and flavor and pour sour cream in it like they had taught me to. You could even buy beer there. So after I finished with the ciorba, I’d buy a dark beer then just sit and watch the college girls in a procession. I never talked to them, which was stupid, but I had the same trouble with girls at home. I’d study their profiles, the European grace with which they strode purposefully, until my beer was finished and I got my deposit back on the bottle.

One day, a chubby girl saw me there watching. She wasn’t really chubby, she was like, American-sized. As soon as I got up, she started coming toward me, and I got scared that she was going to curse me out for being a perv, so I walked faster. But I could hear her heels behind me click-clacking. So I sped up. Then she sped up. I walked up Copou Hill, she click-clacked up Copou Hill gaining on me, past the park where there were crews coming along cutting the grass with scythes. To lose her, I dipped into this bar with pool tables. A lot of students went there. But I wasn’t very good at pool, so I just bought another beer and found a corner to hide in.

She came in after me and bought a coffee from the bar and found a table she could sit at and watch me. It was hard to tell exactly what she was doing because I kept my head down so as not to make eye contact. She got out some papers and pretended to do her homework or study. I got out some papers I had from students that I was supposed to be grading. But I was too nervous to grade them, so I just kept reshuffling the papers and would occasionally make marks on a piece of scratch paper, so it looked like I was actually busy.

Theoretically, you could say it was out of loyalty. I was dating another American Peace Corps girl in a town three hours away by bus. I felt sorry for her. She was stuck in Dorohoi, a shithole in the Moldovan countryside. I knew if I went up there for the weekend we would fuck. So I’d go up there once a month and we’d get drunk and fuck all weekend, just casual shitty sex before we’d pass out.

I went up there for Thanksgiving the year before with some other volunteers. She fucked up the turkey and I tried to make my potato surprise. Somebody made a dish with apples, but they came out blue and we weren’t sure if it was safe to eat them. Eventually, we got hungry and ate them anyway. Raven and I weren’t really dating, at least in my mind. She was a girl that I fucked with from time to time. So the loyalty argument was really an excuse.

But this mystery girl, even if we were to talk, what could we do? I wasn’t interested, and even if I were, there was nowhere to bring her. What was I going to do, take her back to my little room covered in icons and quiltwork at my host family’s flat? Let her get cross-examined by my host mother? It wouldn’t be fair. I suppose we could have gone to Copou Park. Sometimes students would make out behind the bushes there. But usually that didn’t happen until after dark and it was still mid afternoon.

I felt more than a little ridiculous. I didn’t really want to be in this bar drinking my third beer of the day at four o’clock in the afternoon. But if I left she might follow me again and it was downhill until the train station from this direction. Which meant that if I walked quickly I’d be practically running by the time I reached the bottom of the hill. And I’d have to pass by the school where I taught. What would my students think, watching their teacher get chased down the hill by a lady in heels, half-drunk in the middle of the afternoon?

In the half light of the pool hall, her long dark hair made her seem more alluring than frightening. Why was she following me? Was she a spy? Would she fuck me in exchange for state secrets I didn’t have? Don’t think, just act, I coached myself. By the time I was halfway to her table I realized she wasn’t there anymore and froze. Then I saw her playing pool with her friends. I couldn’t hold a conversation with a whole group of students. I could barely manage a one on one conversation without music playing in the background under the best of circumstances in my shitty Romanian pidgin dialectics. This was a mishmash of overheard sayings, idioms like o brunza buna in burdof de caine I had learned from my students, one of whom compared me to ‘a good cheese wrapped in a dog’s bladder,’ halfwitted ejaculations taught me by my previous host family in the mountains, and swears I had picked up by living between two football pitches behind the school. I would relate them in a slurry of Texas twanged Moldovan accented Romanian that happened in a world where time existed exclusively in the present and I repeatedly bounced back and forth between the formal and casual forms of verbs because I couldn’t tell what the conversation called for.

Luckily, she rescued me from my state of indecision by returning to grab a lighter from her coat pocket. I sauntered over the remaining three feet as casually as possible and asked her, “I see you are a player of the billiards?”

“Oh no, just saying hi to some friends.”

“Ah, ma’m, I also am not a player of the billiards.”

“Very good.”

“Why did you come here, ma’m if not to play of the billiards?”

“I might ask you the same.” She smiled and lit her cigarette.

I deeply regretted finishing my beer and not having a prop or any sort of stage business to do with my hands.

“My name is Nathan. I teach of the English.”

She switched over to English, mercifully.

“I am Alexandra,” she said and offered me her hand.

I reached out to shake it where it remained limp.

She laughed.

“Professor Nathan, it is customary to kiss a lady’s hand in Romania, not shake it.”

“Still?” I asked, confused. Then switching back to Romanian, “I had thought that this was had been done how they were the gentlemen to do in the past with the hand of a lady.”

“Then too,” she said and exhaled her cigarette smoke daintily.

“Always are saying the men I kiss of your hand, but never are they kissing of the hand of the lady.” I offered. Sara mana, was a common saying in the teacher’s lounge.

“Yes, it is true there are not so many that are gentlemen’s in the time we have of now living in” she offered in a grand gesture of English.

A pause stood between us. I became aware of her friends' eyes on us, their game of billiards having ground to a screeching halt.

“Is it possible, I can buy you something to drink?”

“No, thank you. I already have a coffee. But that is very beautiful of you Professor Nathan.”

Romanian girls do not drink with strange men unless they are dating, so her refusal wasn’t as hopeless as it seemed.

“Well, I should be going,” I said, not knowing how long I could resist in such a conversation with an audience.

“Do you have somewhere to be?” she asked, amused by my stalling.

“I am going to the home where I am living” I managed.

“You have a lot of papers to grade I see.”

“Yes, yes, many of the papers, mine, students of mine have to be writing, for to be graded in this evening, by myself.”

“Yes, that is the teacher’s life. My mother is a teacher.”

“Ah, what does teaching your mother?”

“Mathematics.”

“Very difficult here, I am seeing the students of mine with the notebooks, so full with the notes, they are making tears of the eyes.”

She laughed, more sympathetically this time.

“Yes, is very difficult the math in Romania,” she responded in English.

“Well, you must to return to your game, are waiting, miss, the friends of yours for you, I am believing.”

“I should return to the game, you are correct Professor Nathan.”

“Perhaps we will be seeing of each other again in day of future?” I ventured.

“That would be quite lovely,” she said, finally tipping her hand.

I bowed stupidly and returned to my table to gather my papers up. I smiled awkwardly on the way out of the bar and was blinded by the fading afternoon sun, surprised by the light after the dimness of the bar.

A connection had been made. I hadn’t chickened out after all. I imagined all her friends laughing at me. On the way home I treated myself to a visine tort and an hour at the internet cafe. I saw pictures of the flooding from Hurricane Katrina. At the train station, I kept staring at the signs flipping over every couple minutes with the new Arrivals and Departures on them, hypnotized. The wind knifed me out of my reveries and I crossed the rickety bridge, knowing that my host family mother would be worried about why I hadn’t come home yet and admonishing Serban and Andrei about their failures to look after the American. I weaved my way through the market stands and bought a rose for her for five lei.

I should have got her phone number at least. Jesus, I’m such an idiot, I thought. I sent Raven a text about coming up to Dorohoi for the weekend. She wrote back right away and I felt better and worse at the same time, imagining force feeding a giant plate of lukewarm blue apple crisp all in one sitting. When I got to the bloc the elevator was broken, so I had to climb the six flights of stairs.

I leaned hard against the wall on the fifth floor, not ready to go in yet. Outside I heard the trolley going by, the useless trolley that zigzagged back and forth across Iasi, not going anywhere anyone actually wanted to go directly, but banging and clanging at every stop. I realized I hadn’t written my own mother back at the internet cafe and felt guilty. I trudged the final flight of stairs up to six. Somehow I couldn’t bear to go in. A few seconds passed and my host family mother opened the door, staring dourly at me.

She lowered her glasses.

“Are you waiting for an invitation?”

“I don’t know”

I didn’t then, and I still don’t now.

Fresh Water, Salt Water

by Chidera Udochukwu-Nduka

My father drew spirals in his notebook. He carved small ones at first before the larger, looser ones that swept across the page like ocean waves. I found the books later, stacked in our basement in Toronto where they had lived for over fifteen years, growing mold in the damp Canadian cold.

“Why spirals, Papa?” I asked him once watching him doodle during my childhood in Owerri.

“It is because nothing moves in straight lines, not people, water or even time.”

He was drawing the Niger Delta without knowing it. From above, the delta is all spirals, creeks branching out into smaller creeks, rivers moving apart and finally rejoining, water that cannot decide whether it wants to be fresh or salty so it becomes both. The Omi River where I grew up flowed in loops, curving back on itself like an ouroboros, like time refusing to be linear. I remember standing on its edge as a child, watching how the current would push forward and backward, never the same water twice but always in the same motion. My father would say that the river was breathing and I agreed because there was no other explanation for that in-out, give-take rhythm of the river.

I was a child who hid at the rivers’ edge. When the boys called me “nwoke-nwanyi” or “girl-boy” because I constantly pushed out my hips when I walked, I had no words to defend myself. When they pulled at my wrapper and pushed me to the ground, I ran to the mangroves and stayed there till evening came. The river hid me without asking me to explain myself. I was eight and it was the first thing that accepted me exactly as I was.

In Vodou cosmology which I would learn much later, the spirit Lasiren moves in spirals. She is the Iwa of the sea and women who refused to be possessed by any other thing apart from water. In my father’s Igbo cosmology, Mamiwata does the same. They are not the same goddess but they understand one universal truth; even though water changes form, it never forgets, the ocean that connects Haiti to West Africa doesn’t care about the human divisions of tribes and nations but it is one body, spiraling around the planet, holding the drowned and all our dreams in the same salt.

This is an essay about spirals, about how Haitian Spiralism made me understand my own Nigerian displacement and about what it means to be queer inside a diaspora that doesn’t have the terms to describe what you are.

My family left Nigeria in 2008 when I was fifteen. My father got a position at a Toronto Hospital. We flew Air France, business class. This was not the same as the Middle Passage but there is something leaving does to you. The ocean is the original archive of Spirality, the first in-between. The same water that carried people from the shores of the Niger Delta and deposited them in other places did not forget what it had held, it kept everything, the names that didn’t survive the crossing, and the different cosmologies that collided and rebuilt themselves on new soil from memory. This is why Mamiwata and Lasiren are two siblings born from different mothers. The Middle Passage did not just move people, it moved their water and their spirits. Spiralism was born from this crossing, and understood displacement and migration are recursive and moving in circles.

And so was I. Time stopped moving when I left and I spent it calculating, what time is it in Lagos now? Is everyone awake?

I kept track with two clocks in my phone, one for Toronto and one for Nigeria. I told myself it would help me now when to call home. This was an evidence of spirality, a proof that time for me, was no longer linear and I was trapped in between two seasons at once, the eternal presence of the diaspora and the transient reminder of home, here and there, now and then.

In Dezafi, Frankétienne wrote about a zombie who regains consciousness and realizes that he has been walking in circles. The zombie cannot escape the plantation because the bokor has drawn a circle of salt around it. He can see the freedom but his feet keep tracing the same path, in a circle, spiral, then return.

I read Dezafi in translation, English from Kreyol, in a Toronto library where I was studying for Pharmacy school like my father, already continuing the loop. The realization of the zombie hit me, the circle of salt felt like a border between Nigeria and everywhere else, between who I was and who I was becoming, the child that played on the banks of Omi River and the one that worked behind a counter in a pharmacy. You can leave home but home keeps pulling you back in concentric circles until you realize you actually never left. You carry home in your body like water in your cells.

In Toronto, I had learnt to switch so smoothly like the maple leaf syrup down your throat. I spoke in my best Canadian intonation at work and took it off with my clothes at home where I talked to my parents in Nigerian accent. I learned to translate not just between tongues but entire world views, explaining to my Canadian friends why I sent money every month and my Nigerian friends why I cannot come home often. I was learning what it meant to be multiple, refusing singularity and embodying the spiral in my daily life. Behind the pharmacy counter, I had learned another type of compounding. In pharmacy, a compound is a substance that does not exist in nature, it is made by combining two or more things into something new that cannot be separated back into its parts. The pill itself is a delta, multiple substances mixed together doing different work in the body.

The Spiral Jetty, Robert Smithson’s land art in Utah’s Great Salt Lake is 1500 feet long and coils counterclockwise into water so salty that nothing can survive in it. When the water is low, you can walk the spiral from the shore to the center, when it floods, the spiral disappears, but remains, waiting beneath the surface.

Up close, it takes fifteen minutes to reach the center and when you get there, there is nothing but rocks and water touching stone, you turn around and walk back out, the same spiral in reverse.

There is something to take out of this; structures exist whether we see them or not, just like diaspora, visible during the low tide and submerged during the high but it is always there, shaping the landscape beneath the surface.

Spiralism as a literary philosophy does the same thing, refusing Western insistence on rising action, climax and resolution. Instead, it offers recursive storytelling, pieces that circle back and unreliable narrators that contradict themselves because that is what people do; contradict themselves. Memory doesn’t move in straight lines. Return doesn’t mean going backward but approaching the same point from a different angle, changed by rotation.

When my Aunty died in 2018, I flew back for the funeral. I flew from Pearson airport to Paris, then to Delta before Owerri, a spiral across the Atlantic, tracing a route that my family had flown a decade before but in reverse.

At the airport, my cousin Ngozi picked me up, she looked at me the way Nigerians looked at people who had been in abroad too long, assessing the weight I had lost and the accent I was trying to hide. She didn’t say much but I had understood, the spiral had brought me back but I wasn’t the same water. On the second day of the funeral, the women danced around her in circles, their feet matching in the same counterclockwise pattern, spiraling inward. They were drawing the spiral around her, keeping her in motion and refusing death as a full stop.

In Igbo cosmology, the dead do not leave, they circle back. They return in children and in dreams. Reincarnation is not a second chance but a continuation, the same soul, coming from a different angle, changed by the rotation of a life lived. Ani, the earth goddess holds both the dead and the unborn in one body. Here, spiral is not a metaphor but theology.

I went back to Omi River and collected water on the last morning before Ngozi drove me back to the airport. I had no plastic container so I bought a small plastic bottle of Eva water, drank it in three swallows and walked down to the river bank alone.

I filled the bottle slowly, watching the water darken as it entered the bottle with its sediments, mangrove smell and the memory of all the legs that had walked on its bank. I sealed it and put in my carry-on.

I kept it on my desk in Toronto, next to a jar of water from Lake Ontario, two bodies of water in glass, between here and there. Sometimes I open the bottles and pour a little of each in my palm. The water from the Lake is clearer and colder, the water from Nigeria is darker and warmer carrying the smell of mangrove dipped in diesel which was my home as it was, not as I remembered it. I mixed them together and watched them become one, just like my hair in my mother’s hands when she braided my hair section by section moving in the same spiral pattern as the rivers, mapping a route across my scalp in the same counterclockwise motion the Spiral Jetty makes, becoming something that could not be separated back into its parts.

This is the spiral; and what it means to be non-binary and become a delta in your own body.

Sometimes, when my mother asks when I am going back, I understand that there is no back, only spiral, the continuous return that is also a departure and the arrival that is a preparation of another journey.

Outside in winter, Lake Ontario is frozen, a vast white sheet that is still water, transformed to ice. In summer, the ice will melt and the water will move again, flowing east towards the Atlantic eventually reaching the Caribbean and touching the shores of both Haiti and Nigeria.

My father was right, nothing moves in straight lines; not water, time or people. Spirality has taught me to stop forcing linearity and stop apologizing for not having a clean trajectory from here to there. It taught me that I can miss Nigeria while loving Toronto and grieve people while celebrating the lives they lived. The spiral insists that you can be the delta, a place where opposites refuse to oppose and multiplicity is not confusion but clarity.

This is what migration is. It is how we survive, not by going forward or backward but by spiraling. By allowing the saltwater and fresh water meet in our bodies and refusing to choose which one we are.

We are the delta.

Border towns don't usually offer the quintessential experience of a country so we decided to move every other year, renting furnished homes and experiencing life in different places until we felt "at home". We'd know we were home when we didn't feel like moving again.

We recently moved to Oaxaca de Juarez, a Spanish colonial city celebrating its 494th year. It is rich in culinary and cultural traditions and is very open to sharing them with visitors and new immigrants. Famous for its Dia de Muertos (day of the dead) celebration and a summer festival called Guelaguetza where regional cultures show off their traditional dances, costumes, and music.

Local organizers announce hikes, cooking classes, social events and language exchanges on social media groups. They freely mix immigrants, visitors, and locals. People are friendly and cooperative, and events are multilingual. Oaxacans are learning English while teaching us Norteños Spanish at language exchange events. I've also noticed how people show concern for one another and offer help freely, showing genuine concern. A real community that feels like home.

AnonymousUkrainian living in Bucharest, Romania

Sunlight through the green leaves of the apple orchard. Grandma who spends her days in the kitchen of a small apartment — not because she has to, but because it is her favorite place. Friends who come to play and share their stories and moments with me. A drawer full of books that I love to explore in any free moment.

All of this makes up the smell and emotion of early childhood — my original Home. A Kyiv home. Five generations of my family in that city.

I left at nineteen. Six countries since. Each time I packed, I carried less of the old place and more of something I couldn't name. I have never felt truly homesick. Not because I don't love where I come from, but because I learned early that I don't belong to one place, but rather to this whole world.

People sometimes ask where I'm from, and the honest answer never fits a single word. I am a Ukrainian in a Romanian city, post-Soviet in the EU, a mother in three languages, an insider nowhere and an observer everywhere.

But I know what I need.

A flower on the windowsill — a red pelargonium, the same kind my grandma grew. Friends and their voices at my fingertips. A book for the quiet moments.

Now it is a Bucharest windowsill. The light falls warmer here, longer. My two older children are already adults, building homes of their own somewhere in the world. My youngest is still shaping what the word home will mean for her.

I hope that flower on the window will be part of it.
